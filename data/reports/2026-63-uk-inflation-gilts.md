**Coverage summary.** Consumer price inflation rose to 3.5% in September from 3.1%, above the 3.4% consensus and the highest in almost a year, driven by motor fuels as severe disruption in the Strait of Hormuz keeps Brent near $110. Core inflation rose to 2.8% and services to 3.7%. On the same morning, public sector borrowing came in at £24.5bn, £1.9bn above the OBR forecast, with debt interest of £10.9bn, the highest for any September on record. Markets now price a quarter-point rise in Bank Rate on 5 November, a week after the Budget on 28 October.

## Executive Summary

**Inflation is turning up again, mainly because of energy.** Motor fuels are 29.8% more expensive than a year ago and transport alone added almost a percentage point to the headline rate. Food inflation, by contrast, fell to 1.3%. The Bank of England expects inflation of about 3.75% in the fourth quarter and slightly above 4% early in 2027, when the household energy price cap is due to rise again.

**The Bank is set to raise rates.** Three of nine policymakers already voted for a hike in July and September, and core and services inflation both beat forecasts. Markets price about a 95% chance of a rise to 4.00% on 5 November, which would be the first increase since August 2023.

**Gilt yields are the highest in the G7.** The 30-year yield passed 6% in early October for the first time since 1998, and the ten-year approached 5.5%. The move is largely global, driven by oil and US Treasuries, but higher borrowing costs feed directly into the debt interest bill.

**The Budget has little room.** The OBR left £23.6bn of headroom in March; UBS estimates higher yields alone have since cost about £10bn. A credible, uneventful Budget is the best outcome for gilts; a loosening would be judged harshly.

## Inflation

Headline inflation has risen for three consecutive months, from 2.6% in June to 3.5% in September. Monthly prices rose 0.4%. Petrol averaged 169.5p a litre and diesel 192.5p, the highest since July 2022, and goods inflation jumped to 3.2% from 2.7%. The worry for the Bank is that core and services prices also accelerated: core rose to 2.8% and services to 3.7%, both above expectations, while factory-gate prices rose 4.6%, the most since April 2023.

**Inflation compared, September 2026 (% year on year)**

- **United Kingdom (CPI)**: headline 3.5, core 2.8, energy: fuels +29.8.
- **United States (CPI)**: headline 3.7, core 2.6, energy +20.4.
- **Euro area (HICP)**: headline 3.8, core 2.5, energy +18.9.

Sources: ONS; US Bureau of Labor Statistics (14 October); Eurostat (16 October).

Unlike in 2022 and 2023, the United Kingdom is not the outlier on headline inflation: the energy shock has lifted prices everywhere, and headline rates in the United States and the euro area are slightly higher. The difference is in core inflation, which at 2.8% is above both, a sign of more persistent domestic price pressure.

## Labour Market and Growth

The labour market is cooling but not breaking. Unemployment was 4.7% in the three months to August, vacancies fell to 691,000 and payrolled employment is 66,000 lower than a year ago. Regular pay growth held at 3.7%, and was 3.0% in the private sector, the measure the Bank watches most closely. Growth has held up better than expected: GDP rose 0.5% in the second quarter and 0.7% in the three months to August. This is not an economy that needs rate cuts, which is why an energy shock tilts the balance towards tightening.

## The Bank of England

Bank Rate has been 3.75% since December 2025, after 1.5 points of cuts from August 2024. This year the vote has swung from easing to tightening: in February four members voted for a cut, but Catherine Mann, Huw Pill and Megan Greene voted for a rise to 4% in July and again in September. Governor Andrew Bailey said in September that policy “may have to tighten” if the Middle East conflict persists, and Greene argued on 13 October that waiting for second-round effects would be too late.

**Monetary Policy Committee votes in 2026**

- **5 Feb**: hold 3.75%, 5-4; 4 members dissented (cut to 3.50%).
- **19 Mar**: hold, 9-0; no dissent.
- **30 Apr**: hold, 8-1; Pill (hike).
- **18 Jun**: hold, 7-2; Greene, Pill (hike).
- **30 Jul**: hold, 6-3; Greene, Mann, Pill (hike).

- **5 Nov**: hike to 4.00% priced, ~95% (OIS).

The Bank has also changed its balance-sheet policy. In September it voted 9-0 to stop selling long-dated gilts until April while it consults on selling directly to the Debt Management Office, and to hold £120bn of the longest bonds permanently. That removes a source of supply at the long end, which Aberdeen described as gilt-positive.

## Gilts

Long gilt yields reached their highest in almost three decades this month. On 7 October the 30-year yield touched 6.04% and the ten-year 5.48% in a global sell-off led by oil and US Treasuries. On the Bank of England’s own curve, the 20-year zero-coupon yield peaked at 6.04% on 8 October, the highest since January 1998. The ten-year was about 5.40% on 21 October, above both US Treasuries (5.10%) and German Bunds (3.41%). UK borrowing costs are the highest in the G7.

Source: Bank of England zero-coupon curve (IUDLNZC, IUDMNZC), daily sampled weekly, to 19 October 2026: 20-year 5.93%, 10-year 5.41%.

This is not a repeat of the September 2022 crisis, when the 30-year yield rose 1.5 points in four days and forced pension funds to sell. The current rise has been gradual and shared with other markets, and there are no reports of stress in liability-driven funds. But the level matters more than the speed: about a quarter of gilts are index-linked, so higher inflation raises the interest bill directly, and every new bond is issued at yields well above the average on existing debt.

## Public Finances

Borrowing in the first half of the fiscal year was £97.8bn, £4.4bn less than a year earlier but £6.0bn above the OBR’s March forecast. Receipts are rising faster than spending, 6.0% against 4.9%, yet interest is eating the improvement: central government debt interest reached £60.8bn in six months, on our calculations from ONS data the highest for the first half of any fiscal year since 1997, in cash terms. Of September’s £10.9bn, £4.0bn reflected the inflation uplift on index-linked gilts.

Source: ONS (series J5II, NMFX), first six months of each fiscal year; Andra Markets calculations. For comparison, first-half net borrowing was £206bn in 2020-21 and £102bn in 2025-26, when debt interest was £59bn.

Net debt passed £3tn for the first time in September, at 94.0% of GDP, slightly lower as a share of output than a year ago because nominal GDP is growing quickly. That is the paradox of the moment: inflation flatters the debt ratio while raising the cost of servicing it. S&P has flagged 100% of GDP as a sensitivity for its AA rating.

## The Budget

The Budget on 28 October will be the first for John Healey, appointed Chancellor when Andy Burnham became Prime Minister on 20 July. Markets have not forgotten Burnham’s talk of fiscal “flexibility” on his first day, when the ten-year yield rose 8 basis points. The fiscal rules leave little room. UBS estimates that of the £23.6bn of headroom in March, higher yields have cost about £10bn and inflation £3bn, partly offset by stronger receipts; lower migration costs a further £4bn on IFS estimates cited by UBS, with £7bn of unfunded policy pressures.

**Erosion of fiscal headroom since March (£bn, estimates)**

- **Headroom, March 2026 forecast**: 23.6 (OBR).
- **Higher gilt yields**: −10 (UBS).
- **Inflation effects (net of receipts)**: −3 (UBS).
- **Lower net migration**: −4 (IFS, cited by UBS).
- **Unfunded policy pressures**: −7 (UBS).
- **Implied headroom before action**: ~0 (Andra Markets arithmetic).
- **Expected tax rises**: 5 to 6 (ING).

Sources: OBR (March 2026); UBS and ING via Investing.com and FXStreet. Estimates are not additive with certainty.

ING expects £5bn to £6bn of tax rises, for example on bank profits or on landlords’ income, and sees the gross financing requirement approaching £325bn in 2027-28. Investors are divided. Fund managers including M&G and Ninety One have been buying gilts ahead of the Budget, and HSBC favours the long end; Franklin Templeton warns the government is “kicking the can down the road”. Barclays’ view sums up the consensus: a boring Budget is the best outcome.

## Energy and Sterling

The common thread is energy. Brent has risen about 79% this year to around $109, and the household energy price cap of £1,723 is expected to rise by 16% or more in January according to Cornwall Insight, which would push inflation above 4% early in 2027. Sterling has been stable, at $1.34 on 21 October, down about 0.5% this year, and the FTSE 100, heavy in energy and mining, is up about 8%. The currency is not signalling a loss of confidence, an important difference from 2022.
