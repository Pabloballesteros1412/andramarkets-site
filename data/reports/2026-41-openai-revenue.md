## Executive Summary

**The revenue gap is mainly about definitions.** The US$70 billion figure was produced by investors trying to compare OpenAI with Anthropic on the same basis. On OpenAI’s own, stricter measure, the run rate is about US$50 billion. That is still more than double the level at the end of 2025, but it is a reminder that the headline numbers behind the AI trade are not audited and are not calculated the same way.

**The real question is cash, not growth.** OpenAI earned US$13.1 billion in 2025 and lost US$20.9 billion at the operating level. Its own plan, reported by the FT, shows about US$278 billion of cash burn between 2026 and 2030 before it turns profitable. Every dollar of that has to come from investors, and much of it flows straight to its suppliers.

**Oracle is the most exposed listed company.** About half of its US$664 billion order backlog is tied to OpenAI, it is funding new data centres with debt, its credit rating was cut to BBB- in July and its shares are down 26 per cent this year. Chipmakers are exposed too, but more indirectly and with a broader base of customers.

## What the Reports Say

On 29 September, Axios reported, citing people familiar with the financials, that OpenAI’s annual recurring revenue was nearing US$70 billion, up more than 70 per cent since the start of the third quarter, with business revenue more than doubling and consumer revenue adding more in the quarter than in all of 2025. Oracle rose 3.9 per cent that day. Today, the FT reported that OpenAI had told investors its annualised revenue was approaching US$50 billion at the end of September. According to the report, investors produced the higher estimates when they tried to compare OpenAI with Anthropic, which includes sales made through cloud partners such as Amazon Web Services and Google Cloud. OpenAI told investors that revenue grew more than 70 per cent over the period, so the growth rate itself has not been disputed.

A run rate is the revenue of a recent month multiplied by twelve. It is useful to show momentum, but it is not reported revenue, it is not audited, and each company decides what to include. For comparison, OpenAI’s own internal plan, reported by the FT in September, foresees revenue of about US$36 billion for the whole of 2026. A September run rate of US$50 billion is consistent with that plan; one of US$70 billion would have implied a sharp acceleration.

## Costs and Cash Burn

OpenAI’s audited figures for 2025, obtained by the writer Ed Zitron and confirmed by the FT in June, show revenue of US$13.07 billion against total costs of about US$34 billion, of which US$19.2 billion was research and development, mostly computing. The operating loss was US$20.9 billion. The net loss of US$38.5 billion was larger because of a US$41.6 billion non-cash accounting charge. In other words, OpenAI spent about US$2.60 for every dollar it earned.

Source: audited 2025 figures reported by Yahoo Finance and the Financial Times (16 to 17 June 2026). The net loss attributable to OpenAI, US$38.5 billion, includes a US$41.6 billion non-cash fair-value charge.

The company’s plan to 2030, from a July presentation reported by the FT, is ambitious: revenue of about US$350 billion in 2030 and cumulative negative free cash flow of about US$278 billion between 2026 and 2030, slightly less than the US$305 billion in the May version. Its computing commitments have been reported at between US$600 billion and US$1.4 trillion depending on the date and definition; the July presentation put spending on computing at about US$856 billion through 2030.

## How OpenAI Reaches the Market

OpenAI is not listed, but its finances reach public markets in three ways. The first is contracts: Oracle, Microsoft, CoreWeave, Amazon and the chipmakers have signed multi-year deals whose value depends on OpenAI raising enough money to pay. The second is shareholdings: Microsoft owns about a quarter of the company, and Nvidia, Amazon and SoftBank invested a combined US$110 billion in its latest funding round, which valued it at US$852 billion in March. The third is sentiment: OpenAI’s growth is the most visible test of whether AI spending will earn a return, so news about it moves the whole AI trade, from chipmakers to power companies.

**Oracle.** Oracle’s remaining performance obligations, its contracted future revenue, reached US$664 billion in September. S&P Global and D.A. Davidson estimate that about half relates to OpenAI. To deliver that capacity, Oracle plans gross capital spending of US$90 to 95 billion this fiscal year after negative free cash flow of US$23.7 billion last year, and raised US$43 billion of debt in fiscal 2026. S&P cut it to BBB-, the lowest investment-grade rating, in July. Its shares, down 26 per cent this year, have become the market’s main barometer of OpenAI’s finances.

**Microsoft.** Microsoft earned US$24.1 billion of revenue from OpenAI arrangements in its fiscal 2026, and OpenAI has committed to buy a further US$250 billion of Azure services. In July, Microsoft’s commercial backlog was US$678 billion, up 84 per cent, but only 25 per cent excluding OpenAI. The exposure is large, but Microsoft is far more diversified and generates the cash to absorb it.

**Chipmakers and cloud providers.** Nvidia replaced a planned US$100 billion arrangement with a US$30 billion equity investment in February. AMD agreed in 2025 to supply 6 gigawatts of GPUs and gave OpenAI warrants for up to 160 million shares. Broadcom is designing 10 gigawatts of custom chips and, according to the WSJ, is arranging more than US$50 billion of financing with Apollo and Blackstone for them. CoreWeave signed an US$11.9 billion contract in March. For these companies, OpenAI is one large customer among several, but it is the one whose spending is least covered by its own cash flow.

## The Revenue Gap

The debate about OpenAI is part of a wider question. Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta plan to spend about US$760 billion on capital investment this year, against US$413 billion in 2025. Bain estimated in September that AI will need about US$6 trillion of annual revenue by 2031 to justify the investment, against about US$1.8 trillion from current uses; Sequoia’s David Cahn puts the revenue needed for this year’s spending at about US$3 trillion. OpenAI and Anthropic, the two largest AI model companies, together report run rates in the range of US$115 to 135 billion depending on the method used. Growth is real and fast, but revenue still covers only a small part of the spending.

A comparison with Anthropic is useful for investors because its IPO prospectus, reported by Reuters in September, gives the first audited view of a frontier AI company: 2025 revenue of about US$4.6 billion, a net loss of about US$42 billion including a US$34 billion accounting charge, and US$518 billion of future computing obligations. Its run rate exceeded US$65 billion in July. Its listing, expected after the November midterm elections, and OpenAI’s planned IPO in 2027 will bring much more disclosure to a market that today depends on leaks.

Our base case is that today’s news is mainly about how revenue is measured. The more important test is whether OpenAI can complete its new funding round, reported by Bloomberg in September to be under discussion at a valuation above US$1.2 trillion, on schedule. A successful round would cover several years of cash burn; a delay or a lower price would raise doubts about contracts signed on the assumption of unlimited funding.

## Risk Considerations

The main risk is concentration: a single private company, with no public accounts, sits behind a large share of the contracted backlog of Oracle and Microsoft and a growing share of chip orders. Data comes from leaks and unaudited run rates that change with definitions, as today shows. Execution risk also matters: on 28 September the WSJ reported that OpenAI cancelled the launch of its GPT-6.1 Astra model after safety tests. On the other hand, competition between AI developers keeps demand for computing high, which supports chipmakers even if one customer slows.

## Outlook and Monitoring Points

Investors should watch OpenAI’s response to the FT report and any update on its funding round; the results of ASML on 14 October and TSMC on 15 October, the first reads on AI chip demand for 2027; Microsoft’s quarterly results at the end of October, which show the OpenAI share of its backlog; Oracle’s credit spreads and bond issuance; and the timetable for Anthropic’s IPO. As long as the money keeps arriving, the AI trade holds; the risk is a pause in funding, not a slowdown in growth.
