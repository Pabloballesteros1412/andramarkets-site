**Coverage summary.** The US housing market enters the fourth quarter with mortgage rates back above 7 per cent, existing-home sales at 3.98 million (annualised), close to a thirty-year low, and the monthly payment on a median-priced home roughly 2.4 times its level of January 2021. Yet prices are not falling at the national level: the Case-Shiller index is up 1.9 per cent on the year, because about half of all mortgage holders pay less than 4 per cent and will not sell, keeping the supply of existing homes tight. The adjustment is happening through volumes, through builders, who are cutting prices and buying down rates to keep selling, and through real prices, which have fallen for fourteen consecutive months. Credit is sound by the standards of 2006, with high borrower credit scores and fixed-rate loans, but cracks are visible in government-insured FHA lending and in apartment loans. This is a frozen market, not a collapsing one: the risk is stagnation, with the weak points in new homes, FHA borrowers and multifamily.

## Executive Summary

**Rates have reset higher.** The ten-year Treasury yield touched about 5.35 per cent intraday in the week of 5 October, its highest since 2002, after the Federal Reserve raised rates in September for the first time in more than three years. The 30-year mortgage rate followed, rising for seven consecutive weeks to 7.40 per cent; the spread between the two remains around 2.2 percentage points, against an average of 1.76 points in 2000 to 2019.

**Affordability is close to its worst in four decades.** On our calculation, the principal and interest payment on the median existing home with a 20 per cent down payment is about US$2,377 a month at today’s rate, against US$991 in January 2021. A household would need an income of about US$95,000 to keep that payment at 30 per cent of income, before taxes and insurance.

**The lock-in effect is keeping prices up and volumes down.** 49.1 per cent of outstanding mortgages carry a rate below 4 per cent and 65.6 per cent below 5 per cent. Owners who would lose those rates do not sell, so existing inventory stays low, transactions collapse and national prices drift sideways rather than fall.

**The pressure shows up elsewhere.** New homes are where prices are adjusting: the median new-home price is down 5.8 per cent on the year on preliminary data, 38 per cent of builders are cutting prices and Lennar’s gross margin has fallen to 15.8 per cent. In credit, FHA delinquencies are at 11.79 per cent and multifamily commercial mortgage delinquencies have overtaken the overall rate. Our base case for 2027 is stagnation: flat nominal prices, falling real prices and transactions near current lows.

## Mortgage Rates and the Ten-Year Yield

The 30-year fixed rate is priced off the ten-year Treasury yield plus a spread that compensates investors in mortgage-backed securities for prepayment risk and for the cost of originating and servicing the loan. In 2026 both parts have moved against borrowers. The rate fell to 5.98 per cent in late February, its first reading below 6 per cent in about three and a half years, helped by the government-sponsored enterprises’ announcement of US$200 billion of mortgage-bond purchases. Since then the ten-year yield has climbed by more than a percentage point, driven by an oil shock, heavy Treasury issuance and weak auctions, and by the Fed’s September increase to 3.75 to 4.00 per cent. The Mortgage Bankers Association’s contract rate reached 7.49 per cent in the week to 2 October, and the Mortgage News Daily index 7.48 per cent on 9 October.

The spread matters as much as the yield. It widened to more than 3 points in 2023, the widest since the 1980s, when the Fed was selling down its holdings and rate volatility was extreme. It had returned to about 2 points by late 2025 and stayed there through the summer despite the enterprises’ purchases. In early October it widened again, to about 2.2 points, as mortgage bonds underperformed Treasuries by 96 basis points in September, their worst month since March 2023 according to Annaly. A return to the pre-2020 average of 1.76 points would by itself lower the mortgage rate by about 0.4 points.

Source: Freddie Mac PMMS and US Treasury via FRED, weekly; last 8 October 2026. Spread is an Andra Markets calculation from the same series.

## The Mortgage-Bond Market and the Fed

The marginal buyer of agency mortgage-backed securities has changed. The Federal Reserve held about US$2.74 trillion at its peak in 2022; it ended quantitative tightening in December 2025 but still lets its mortgage holdings run off, at about US$15 to 18 billion a month, and reinvests the principal in Treasury bills. Its holdings were US$1.914 trillion on 10 September. Banks (US$2.7 trillion, up 2 per cent) and foreign investors (US$1.4 trillion, up 6.9 per cent) have filled part of the gap, and Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac have nearly doubled their retained portfolios under the purchase programme. But banks are price-sensitive buyers, and when rates rise quickly mortgage holders hedge by selling Treasuries, which reinforces the move: open interest in SOFR swap futures used for such hedging hit a record in September.

**Agency mortgage-backed securities: holders and flows**

- **Fed holdings**: $1.914tn (10 Sep), from ~$2.74tn peak (2022); runoff ~$15-18bn a month.
- **US depository institutions**: $2.7tn (Q2), +2.0% y/y; largest holder.
- **Foreign investors**: $1.4tn (Q2), +6.9% y/y; growing.
- **Fannie Mae retained portfolio**: $173.4bn (Jul), +97.9% y/y; $200bn purchase plan.
- **Freddie Mac retained portfolio**: $139.7bn (Jul), +31.9% y/y; cap raised to $225bn each.
- **Net agency issuance, Jan-Aug**: $46.5bn, against $122.1bn a year earlier; low supply of new loans.
- **Current-coupon MBS spread**: ~125 bp (end-Sep), +13 bp in September; AGNC measure.

## Affordability

The monthly principal and interest payment on the median sale price of all homes sold, assuming a 20 per cent down payment, roughly doubled between 2021 and 2023 and has not come down since: prices kept rising slowly while rates stayed high. Using the median existing-home price of US$429,100 from the National Association of Realtors, the payment at 7.40 per cent is US$2,377, about 140 per cent higher than at the January 2021 combination of a 2.74 per cent rate and a US$303,900 median price. Property taxes and insurance, which have risen fastest in Florida, California and Texas, come on top. Zillow estimates that the typical payment takes 34.3 per cent of median household income, and the Atlanta Fed’s affordability index fell to 68 in July, its lowest in 21 years, according to Fed Governor Barr. Both readings predate the move above 7 per cent.

## The Lock-In Effect

The defining feature of this cycle is that most owners financed or refinanced in 2020 and 2021 at rates of 2.5 to 4 per cent. Selling means giving up that loan and borrowing again at 7.4 per cent, which for a typical loan raises the payment by several hundred dollars a month for the same house. According to the Federal Housing Finance Agency’s National Mortgage Database, 49.1 per cent of outstanding mortgages carried a rate below 4 per cent in the second quarter of 2026, down from 52.5 per cent a year earlier, and 22.5 per cent were above 6 per cent, up from 19.7 per cent. The lock-in is fading, but slowly: at the pace of the past year, it would take about a decade for loans above 6 per cent to become the majority. Only 2.8 per cent of homes changed hands in 2025, according to Redfin. Portable and assumable mortgages, floated by the administration in November 2025 to unlock this supply, have not been implemented.

**Outstanding US mortgages by interest rate, Q2 2026 (% of loans)**

- Below 4%: 49.1%
- 4% to 5%: 16.5%
- 5% to 6%: 11.9%
- 6% and above: 22.5%

Source: FHFA National Mortgage Database via Calculated Risk (Q2 2026); intermediate buckets derived by Andra Markets from cumulative shares (below 4%: 49.1%, below 5%: 65.6%).

Locked-in owners are not distressed owners. Mortgage holders’ equity reached a record US$18 trillion in the second quarter according to ICE, of which US$11.7 trillion can be borrowed against while keeping 20 per cent equity, about US$212,000 per borrower. Rather than move, owners are borrowing against their homes: home-equity lines of credit have risen by US$142 billion since early 2022 to US$459 billion, while mortgage balances fell by US$74 billion in the second quarter and total household debt declined for the first time since 2020.

## Transactions and Inventory

Existing-home sales fell to 3.98 million at an annual rate in August, a pace consistent with the weakest years since the mid-1990s and well below the 5 to 5.5 million typical of 2015 to 2019. The leading indicators point lower: pending sales were down 4.7 per cent on the year in August, Zillow’s newly pending sales fell 8.5 per cent in September, and mortgage purchase applications were down 15 per cent on the year in early October. Refinancing applications fell 56 per cent. Existing inventory has risen to 1.62 million, or 4.9 months of sales, up from very low levels but still below the six months usually considered balanced. First-time buyers were 30 per cent of August sales, and their median age reached a record 40 in NAR’s 2025 survey; all-cash buyers made up 27 per cent.

The new-home market is in a different position. Builders have kept building, so 483,000 new homes were for sale in August, equal to 8.5 months of sales. That is above the 2010s average but below the 10 to 12 months reached in 2008 and 2009, and only a minority of it is finished, unsold homes. New-home sales rose 6.4 per cent in August to 684,000, helped by incentives, but the median new-home price is now below the median existing-home price, an unusual reversal that shows where prices are adjusting.

Source: US Census Bureau and HUD (MSACSR) via FRED, monthly since 2000; last August 2026.

## House Prices

National prices have slowed sharply but not fallen. The S&P Cotality Case-Shiller National index rose 1.9 per cent in the year to July, the FHFA index 2.6 per cent and ICE’s index 1.5 per cent; Zillow’s value index was up about 1 per cent in September. With consumer prices rising 3.4 per cent, real house prices have fallen for fourteen consecutive months. The national figure hides a sharp regional split. The Northeast and Midwest, where supply is tightest and builders are least active, are still rising: Chicago is up 6.9 per cent and New York 5.8 per cent. The Sun Belt and the West, where builders added the most homes, are softer: Seattle, Las Vegas and Denver are down, and 17 of the 50 largest metropolitan areas are falling on Zillow’s measure, including Dallas, Houston, Austin, Tampa, Phoenix and Atlanta. 27.4 per cent of listings had a price cut in September.

**House prices by region and metro (% change on a year earlier)**

- **Case-Shiller metros (Jul)**: rising, Chicago +6.9, New York +5.8, Cleveland +4.2; falling, Seattle −1.6, Las Vegas −1.3, Denver −1.1.
- **NAR median by region (Aug)**: rising, Northeast +4.3, Midwest +3.3; falling or flat, South +0.7, West −0.2.
- **FHFA divisions (Jul)**: Middle Atlantic +6.3; Mountain +0.6 (weakest).
- **Zillow metros (Sep)**: 33 of the 50 largest rising; 17 of 50 falling, concentrated in the Sun Belt and the West.

## Homebuilders

Builders are the shock absorber of this market. Because they cannot wait for rates to fall without carrying inventory, they cut prices and pay to buy down buyers’ mortgage rates, often to below 5 per cent. The cost of that buy-down rises with market rates; Lennar’s management said on its September results call that buy-downs are becoming more expensive. The National Association of Home Builders’ confidence index fell to 32 in September, 38 per cent of builders cut prices, by 6 per cent on average, and 66 per cent used incentives. Margins are compressing at the volume builders that sell to first-time buyers, while luxury builders such as Toll Brothers, whose buyers rely less on mortgages, are holding up better. Construction costs add pressure: tariffs on lumber, cabinets and furniture and a median 6.7 per cent rise in building-material prices, and stricter immigration enforcement in an industry where immigrants make up more than a quarter of the workforce. Homebuilder shares reflect this: the iShares US Home Construction ETF is near its 52-week low and down 14 per cent over a year.

Housing starts were 1.275 million at an annual rate in August. Single-family starts, at 918,000, are holding up because builders are finishing projects already under way, but multifamily starts fell 21.7 per cent in the month as apartment developers face high vacancy and expensive finance. Completions fell to 1.128 million, a seven-year low, which means less new supply in 2027: a source of support for prices and rents later, but a drag on residential investment now.

## Rents and Shelter Inflation

Rents tell a different story from prices. A record wave of apartment completions in 2024 and 2025 pushed vacancy to 7 to 8 per cent depending on the measure (Apartment List 7.0 per cent, Apartments.com 7.8 per cent, CoStar 8.2 per cent), and new-lease rents have stalled: Apartment List shows a 0.4 per cent fall on the year in September, Apartments.com a 0.8 per cent rise and Zillow 2.7 per cent. Shelter in the consumer price index, which has a weight of about 35 per cent and reflects existing leases with a lag of around a year, is rising more slowly than in 2023 but still above market rents. That lag is important for the Fed: shelter disinflation will continue into 2027 even if rates stay high, and the collapse in multifamily starts suggests that rent pressure could return from 2028 as the supply pipeline empties.

## Mortgage Credit and the Comparison with 2006

Mortgage credit is in far better shape than before the 2008 crisis. Since 2022, about 60 to 65 per cent of loans guaranteed by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac have gone to borrowers with credit scores above 750, against about 42 to 44 per cent in 2005 to 2008. Almost all loans are fixed-rate and fully documented, and adjustable-rate mortgages are only about 10 per cent of applications against about 45 per cent of origination volume in 2005. Households spend about 5.8 per cent of disposable income on mortgage payments, against 9.0 per cent in 2007, because the stock of debt was refinanced at low rates. The conventional delinquency rate is 2.72 per cent.

The weak points are at the edges. Delinquencies on FHA loans, which serve borrowers with low down payments and lower credit scores, reached 11.79 per cent in the second quarter, up 122 basis points in a year. ICE data show foreclosure starts up 28.8 per cent and foreclosure inventory up 41 per cent on the year in August, from low levels. The share of Fannie Mae loans with a loan-to-value ratio above 90 per cent is 24.6 per cent, against 6.5 per cent before the crisis, which leaves recent buyers exposed if prices fall in the regions where they are already declining. In commercial real estate, Trepp’s multifamily delinquency rate rose to 8.04 per cent in September, slightly above the overall CMBS rate of 8.02 per cent.

## Policy

Housing policy has focused on the mortgage spread. In January the administration had Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac buy up to US$200 billion of mortgage bonds, and the Treasury Secretary has tied any offering of Fannie and Freddie shares to tighter mortgage spreads. The effect was brief: the spread fell on the announcement and then settled back near 2 points. The plan to sell part of the enterprises and end their conservatorship has been repeatedly delayed; in June the President said a share sale was still under consideration but that there was no rush. Any perception that the implicit government guarantee could weaken would widen spreads and raise mortgage rates, which is why we expect any exit to be gradual. The 50-year mortgage proposal of November 2025 was dropped. On the supply side, Section 232 tariffs on lumber, cabinets and furniture and stricter immigration enforcement raise building costs and lengthen construction times, working against the stated goal of more affordable homes.

## Macroeconomic Transmission

Residential investment is about 3.6 per cent of GDP, close to half its share in 2005 and 2006, so even a sharp fall would subtract less from growth than in 2007 to 2009. Household housing equity is US$35.8 trillion and flat on the year, so the housing wealth effect is neutral rather than negative. The main channels to the wider economy are labour mobility, as locked-in owners do not move for jobs, durable-goods spending linked to home sales, such as furniture and appliances, and the mortgage industry itself, where the MBA expects originations of about US$2.1 trillion in both 2026 and 2027 with rates near current levels. The Fed’s September minutes describe financing conditions for mortgage borrowers as “somewhat restrictive”. For markets, housing is a channel through which higher long-term yields slow the economy and eventually help bring them down.

## Scenarios for 2027

Forecasts published before the ten-year yield crossed 5 per cent are already out of date: in mid-September, Fannie Mae still expected the mortgage rate to fall to 6.8 per cent by the end of 2026, while the MBA’s latest forecast keeps rates near current levels, above 7 per cent, over its horizon. Our scenarios are set by the ten-year yield and the spread. In the base case the yield stays between 5.0 and 5.3 per cent and the spread near 2.1 points, and the market stays frozen. A relief scenario requires the Fed to stop raising rates and the oil shock to fade. A stress scenario combines higher yields with rising unemployment and is the one in which forced sales, rather than lock-in, set prices.

Two things would change our view. A decline in the ten-year yield towards 4.5 per cent combined with a narrower spread would bring the mortgage rate into the low 6s, the level at which applications recovered in early 2026. Conversely, rising unemployment would turn locked-in owners into forced sellers, which is the only route to a broad national price decline. Until then, the US housing market is likely to remain what it is today: expensive, illiquid and stable in nominal prices.
