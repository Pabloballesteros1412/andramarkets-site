**Coverage summary.** The front-month Dutch TTF contract traded at €100.37 per megawatt-hour on 28 October, hours after EU Energy Commissioner Dan Jørgensen warned that Europe faces another energy crisis. The benchmark has risen from €31.51 on the eve of the war, more than three times. EU storage was 74.6% full on 24 October, about 14 points below the five-year seasonal average and the lowest for the date since records began in 2009. Converted into dollars, Europe now pays about $33 per million British thermal units, against about $2.8 at Henry Hub in the United States. The market is not short of gas today; it is short of the buffer it needs to get through a cold February.

## Executive Summary

**Europe never refilled.** Storage stood at just 28.9% in mid-March, when the war closed Hormuz and Qatar halted LNG output. Without Qatari supply the bloc reached only 70.9% at the end of September and 74.6% by 24 October, with daily changes now hovering around zero.

**The direct loss is small, the indirect one is not.** Middle Eastern LNG was only about 8% of EU LNG imports in 2025. Asia lost far more, and both regions now compete for the same Atlantic cargoes. The US now supplies roughly 30% of all EU gas.

**The winter arithmetic is tight.** ENTSOG warned on 8 October that storage could fall well below 30% by the end of winter if LNG stays scarce, and IEEFA estimates the EU may need to cut winter demand by about 7%.

**Brussels is preparing, not yet rationing.** Jørgensen has asked governments to sustain injections, reduce demand voluntarily, aim for 80% storage rather than 90% and design any price support with care.

**Forecasters sit below the market.** Goldman Sachs has a year-end forecast of €70 with a stress level near €105; Citi sees the winter strip at €60 to €70 without escalation and up to €154 if LNG facilities are hit.

**Our reading.** The floor under prices is set by storage, not sentiment. Temperatures and the pace of US arrivals will decide whether €100 is a peak or a waypoint. Industry, which cuts output, is the release valve; households feel it with a lag.

## How We Got Here

The war that began on 28 February closed the Strait of Hormuz, through which about a fifth of global LNG normally flows, and forced Qatar to shut the world’s largest liquefaction complex. TTF rose more than 60% in March, its biggest monthly gain since 2021. It eased into the spring as milder weather and strong renewables curbed demand, then tightened again over the summer as it became clear that Gulf exports would not return quickly. Only 3 LNG carriers crossed Hormuz in August against 18 in July. Oil followed the same path: Brent spot, which stood at $71 on 27 February, has spent most of the autumn above $110.

## Storage

Storage is Europe’s shock absorber. In a normal year it enters November around 90% full and draws down through the winter. This year the EU relaxed its mandatory target, so that 80% reached between 1 October and 1 December counts as compliance, and the Commission has encouraged members to use that flexibility to avoid panic buying. Even the lower bar is now out of reach for many. Germany, which holds about a fifth of EU capacity, was 61% full on 23 October, against roughly 77% a year earlier. Wood Mackenzie’s central case assumes a normal winter and the return of Qatari and Emirati LNG in early 2027; a cold winter could push storage much lower.

Sources: S&P Global Platts (GIE data, 16 Mar and 28 Sep); TFTC (GIE AGSI+, 7 Oct); Bruegel (24 Oct); Trading Economics and discoveryalert.com (seasonal averages, ~86% mid-October and ~89% late October).

## The Global LNG Contest

The Gulf shock is a global LNG problem before it is a European one. Qatar exported 82.4 million tonnes of LNG in 2025, of which only about 8.9% went to Europe; it supplied a quarter of Asia’s imports. With those cargoes gone, buyers in Japan, Korea and Taiwan bid for Atlantic supply, and S&P Global reported on 3 September that the JKM benchmark had reached its highest since December 2022 as Europe outbid Asia for US volumes. The result is an enormous transatlantic spread: US gas at Henry Hub trades near $2.8 per million British thermal units because the US is self-sufficient, while Europe pays around $33. That spread is what pulls record US LNG exports across the Atlantic.

Qatari LNG cargoes in the first six months of the war:

- **Mar–Aug 2025**: 509 cargoes
- **Mar–Aug 2026**: 18 cargoes, −96%

Two factors cap the upside. US exporters shipped record volumes in 2026, and as BASF chief executive Markus Kamieth has noted, China switches from gas to coal as soon as prices rise, releasing LNG to other buyers. He also pointed out that Hormuz removes only about 3% of world gas supply, against roughly 20% of oil. The price required to balance the market is high, but finite.

## The Policy Response

On 27 September Jørgensen wrote to the 27 energy ministers describing a price crisis linked to a supply crisis. He asked them to sustain injections or reduce gas and electricity demand for as long as necessary, to target 80% storage and to remember that voluntary demand reduction worked in 2022. On 8 October the Commission warned that broad energy subsidies could sustain demand and push prices higher. National emergency plans, with interruptible contracts and fuel switching, remain in reserve, and Brussels is seeking powers to order electricity demand cuts in a crisis. From January the EU ban on Russian LNG removes a supply source that partly filled the gap this year.

## Who Is Exposed

Energy-intensive industry is the first adjustment mechanism. BASF’s chief executive has called this probably the most difficult period for the chemical industry in 25 years; the group is cutting fixed costs in its core business by up to 20%, and its Ludwigshafen home site is loss-making. Fertilisers, glass, steel and ceramics face the same arithmetic: when gas costs triple, output is cut before customers are lost.

Power prices have risen far less than gas, up 22.8% between February and August against 88.4% for gas, according to Eurelectric, because renewables now set more hours of the merit order. But gas still sets the price at peak times, and ICIS found coal-fired generation had become more economical than gas. Households feel the shock with a lag: the UK price cap is forecast to rise a further 16% in January according to Cornwall Insight, and EU diesel prices rose 35% between late February and early October.

Source: Eurelectric, 30 September 2026, via Renewable Matter and Admiral Markets.

## 2022 vs 2026

It is tempting to compare this winter with 2022, when Russia cut pipeline supply. Then Europe lost its largest supplier, about 40% of its gas, and TTF peaked near €340 in August; this year the direct loss is small but the global LNG market is short, so Europe pays to compete rather than to replace. In 2022 storage still reached about 95% by November thanks to record injections; this year it is stuck near 75%. The price is a third of the 2022 peak, but the physical buffer is weaker.

## Forecasts
