- **$230bn**: record backlog, June 2026
- **$80.8bn**: 2026 sales guidance (midpoint)
- **$512**: share price, 24% below March record
- **16.5x**: forward earnings, below the S&P 500

Lockheed Martin has never had more work: backlog jumped to $230bn in the second quarter, and since then it has won the bulk of a $58.6bn contract to triple Patriot interceptor production. Yet the shares have lagged, because the company lost about $3.6bn on fixed-price programmes in 2024 and 2025, F-35 deliveries have slowed and Washington is pressing contractors to spend on capacity rather than buybacks.

**Our view:** the demand cycle is the strongest in decades and the valuation is undemanding, but execution, not orders, now decides the share price.

Quarterly sales have grown from about $17bn to $20bn in two years. ## Programme Losses

Lockheed has taken large losses on contracts where it agreed a fixed price for work that proved harder than expected, most of them on two classified programmes. Reserves of about $1.5bn are still booked against further losses, and the company warns that more “could be material”. No new programme losses were reported in the second quarter of 2026.

- **Q4 2024**: MFC and Aeronautics classified programmes, $1.72bn

## Business Segments

Aeronautics, dominated by the F-35, is the largest segment but earns the lowest margin. Missiles and Fire Control is the most profitable and the fastest growing, with backlog nearly doubling in one quarter to $88bn.

## The F-35

The F-35 accounts for about 27% of Lockheed’s sales. Deliveries were halted for a year while a software upgrade (TR-3) was certified, then hit a record 191 jets in 2025; in the first half of 2026 they fell to 51. The US bought 47 jets in 2026, and of the 85 requested for 2027, only 32 are in the base budget. Abroad, Switzerland trimmed its order from 36 to 30 jets and Spain ruled the jet out, while Canada’s review is still open.

## Missiles and Air Defence

Wars in Ukraine and the Middle East have used up interceptors faster than they can be built. CSIS estimates that the Iran war used up about two thirds of US Patriot interceptors and more than a third of THAAD interceptors. Lockheed is the main beneficiary: in July it won a $58.6bn multiyear contract to roughly triple Patriot PAC-3 production, from about 600 a year, by 2030, and in June a $35bn contract to quadruple THAAD output.

- **PAC-3 MSE multiyear (Jul 2026)**: $58.6bn; triple by 2030
- **THAAD (Jun 2026)**: $35bn; quadruple production
- **GMLRS rockets**: $3bn; sustained output

Sources: Lockheed Martin, CSIS via Military Times. The $53.9bn July portion of the PAC-3 award came after the quarter and is not in the $230bn backlog.

## Space and Golden Dome

Space is Lockheed’s smallest segment but a growing one: it builds missile-warning satellites, the Orion capsule that carried the Artemis II crew and GPS satellites. The Golden Dome missile shield, which the Pentagon costs at $185bn (the CBO estimates far more over 20 years), is the largest new opportunity: Lockheed is one of the companies developing space-based interceptor prototypes and has won a missile-tracking satellite award worth more than $1bn. Most of the 2027 funding depends on a budget bill that Congress has not passed.

## The Defence Budget

The administration has asked for $1.5tn for defence in 2027, but $350bn of that depends on a reconciliation bill, and Congress’s budget resolutions include only $60bn for defence. The 2027 year began on a stopgap bill to 11 December that excludes the multiyear munitions contracts the White House requested. Outside the US, NATO members have agreed to spend 5% of GDP on defence by 2035, but Europe is steering more of that money to European suppliers.

- **FY2027 request**: $1.5tn ($1.15tn base + $350bn reconciliation)
- **Continuing resolution**: to 11 Dec 2026; no munitions multiyears
- **Golden Dome**: ~$185bn Pentagon estimate; $17.9bn FY27 ask
- **NATO target**: 5% of GDP by 2035 (3.5% core)
- **EU SAFE loans**: €150bn; non-EU content capped at 35%

Sources: Military Times, CSIS, Breaking Defense, NATO, European Commission.

## Share Price

The shares rose 31% in January on the call for a $1.5tn budget and reached a record of $677 at the close on 2 March, after US strikes on Iran. They have since given back most of the gain and are up only about 6% this year, as the budget stalled in Congress, first-quarter results disappointed and investors moved to faster-growing technology stocks.

## Valuation and Capital Returns

At 16.5 times expected earnings, Lockheed trades below the S&P 500 and well below RTX, with a free cash flow yield of about 6% on 2026 guidance. The dividend was raised for the 24th consecutive year, to $3.60 a quarter, a yield of about 2.8%, now well below the 10-year Treasury yield. Buybacks stopped in the first half of 2026: a January executive order restricts buybacks and dividends at contractors judged to be underperforming, and the company is investing in missile capacity instead.

**Indicators to watch**

- **Programme charges**: any new reach-forward loss
- **F-35 deliveries**: below 150 in 2026

## Verdict

**Strong demand, fair price, execution risk.** Moderately positive: a reasonable hold with modest upside; the key test is the Q3 results on 22 October.

Lockheed has the order book of a wartime contractor and the valuation of a slow-growth industrial. If it delivers the missile ramp without new losses, the shares can recover towards their March level; one more large charge would keep them in the range of the past two years.
