**Coverage summary.** The order signed on 16 October suspends every operative Russia provision of the Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, a law passed by 86 votes to 11 in the Senate and signed by the president only a month earlier. It removes the threat of tariffs of up to 100% on the largest buyers of Russian oil, of sanctions on Sberbank, VTB and the Bank of Russia, and of measures against the shadow fleet. It follows a deal, announced on 9 October, under which Russia will ship diesel to the United States. Treasury Secretary Bessent insists that “no sanctions have been lifted”; Kyiv calls the waiver “a gift” to Moscow.

- **US diesel, 12 Oct**: $6.18/gal, vs $3.48 in January (EIA)
- **Senate vote on the Act**: 86 to 11, waived one month after signing

## Executive Summary

**A waiver, not a lifting.** Washington has not removed a single existing sanction. It has prevented a much tougher set of measures from starting, and given itself an open-ended pause. For Russia, the main gain is the end of the threat to its customers: India and China no longer face US tariffs for buying Russian crude.

**The motive is the oil price.** With the Iran war cutting flows through the Strait of Hormuz to about 11 million barrels a day and US diesel above $6 a gallon, the administration wants every available barrel on the market. Analysts doubt the effect: Goldman Sachs estimates that the Russian diesel deal lowers prices by only $1 to $2 a barrel.

**Europe is moving the other way.** The EU added its largest ever set of Russia listings on 12 October and keeps its price cap at $44.10. The transatlantic split is now the main risk for European defence stocks and for Ukraine’s financing.

**Markets barely moved.** On Monday crude and diesel futures eased, the rouble firmed and European defence shares and banks with Russian exposure slipped, but no move exceeded about 3%. The decision that matters comes after the 60-day review, in mid-December.

## What the order changes, and what it does not

- **Tariffs of up to 100% on top buyers of Russian oil and gas** (Graham Act, §113): waived before taking effect
- **Tariffs of up to 500% on Russian goods** (Graham Act, §112): waived
- **Sanctions on Sberbank, VTB, Gazprombank, Bank of Russia** (Graham Act): waived
- **Shadow-fleet tankers and their insurers** (Graham Act): waived
- **Ban on new US investment and Russian sovereign debt** (Graham Act): waived (in force since 18 Sep)
- **Rosneft and Lukoil designations, Oct 2025** (EO 14024): still in force
- **US imports of Russian diesel** (OFAC General License 135): allowed until 7 Apr 2027
- **G7/EU crude price cap** (G7, EU dynamic cap): $44.10, frozen by the EU
- **EU sanctions, about 4,600 listings** (EU Council): unchanged; 1,569 added on 12 Oct

Sources: White House; ABC News; RFE/RL; Squire Patton Boggs and Clyde & Co (legal analysis); OFAC; European Commission; EU Council. The Act allows tariffs of up to 500% on Russian goods (§112) and up to 100% on third-country buyers (§113).

## The Order

The order was signed late on Friday, after US markets had closed, and posted on the White House website that evening. It certifies to Congress that waiving the Act is “in the national interests of the United States” and lasts until the administration decides that “further action is appropriate”. It also waives the clause that kept existing sanctions in place, which leaves the president free to ease them later without a vote. Secretary of State Rubio said on Saturday that the president wants “the 60-day period of time” to analyse what imposing sanctions and tariffs would mean for the global and US economies.

The waiver overrides a rare bipartisan consensus. The Act passed the Senate by 86 votes to 11 and the House by 262 to 159, and it carries the name of Senator Lindsey Graham, who died in July. Republicans including Senators Tillis and Cornyn criticised the decision; a bill to block the diesel deal was introduced on 16 October. Because the waiver cannot be blocked by a resolution of disapproval, Congress would need a new law, which the president could veto.

## The Diesel Deal

The waiver is the second half of a deal announced on 9 October. Russia is to ship more than 300,000 tonnes of diesel to the United States immediately, 500,000 tonnes in November, then 1 million tonnes and up to 3 million tonnes later, 4.8 million in all, and the Treasury has licensed such imports until April 2027. The reason is visible in prices: US diesel averaged $6.18 a gallon on 12 October, against $3.48 in January, and Kpler estimates a global diesel shortfall of about 1 million barrels a day.

The deal is small next to the problem. Goldman Sachs estimates that the October and November shipments would lower US Gulf Coast and European prices by only $1 to $2 a barrel, against about $11 for a 50-million-barrel release of European reserves; even the full 4.8 million tonnes would cover only about ten days of US demand. Neil Atkinson, a former IEA official, put it more bluntly: “It does not even touch the sides.” Russia’s own diesel exports are constrained, too: the Ukrainian military says its strikes have taken about 45% of Russian refining capacity offline.

## Sanctions Since 2022

Since the invasion of February 2022, Western sanctions have tightened in stages: the exclusion of Russian banks from SWIFT, the freezing of about €210bn of central-bank assets in Europe, the G7 price cap on oil, EU embargoes and, in October 2025, US sanctions on Rosneft and Lukoil. The Graham Act would have been the most far-reaching step yet, because it targeted the buyers of Russian oil rather than Russia itself.

- **Feb-Mar 2022**: SWIFT exclusions; central-bank reserves frozen
- **Dec 2022**: G7 price cap on Russian crude at $60
- **Oct 2025**: US sanctions on Rosneft and Lukoil
- **Dec 2025**: EU freezes Russian assets indefinitely
- **Feb 2026**: EU/UK dynamic cap lowered to $44.10
- **Mar 2026**: US eases some oil restrictions after the Iran war begins
- **Sep 2026**: Graham Act signed (18 Sep)
- **9 Oct 2026**: US-Russia diesel deal; OFAC GL 135
- **12 Oct 2026**: EU adds 1,569 listings, its largest ever
- **16 Oct 2026**: US waives the Graham Act

Sources: EU Council; European Commission; OFAC; Bloomberg; Forbes; Wikipedia (dates of earlier measures).

## The Oil Balance

The supply shock in the Gulf dwarfs anything Russia can add. The IEA estimates flows through the Strait of Hormuz at about 10.9 million barrels a day in September, against about 17 million before the war according to tanker-tracking data, and global stocks 509 million barrels below their February level. Russia exports about 6.5 million barrels a day of crude and products, its lowest since 2018 according to the Kyiv School of Economics, and its crude output is 1.5 million barrels a day below its OPEC+ target because of drone damage.

The waiver therefore does not add barrels so much as reduce risk for those who already buy them. Without the threat of US tariffs, Indian and Chinese refiners can keep taking Russian crude, and the shadow fleet avoids a wave of new designations. Kpler expects easing shadow-fleet sanctions to have virtually no impact on mainstream shipping capacity.

## Russia’s Economy

Russia needs the relief. Oil and gas tax revenue fell 17% in the first nine months of 2026, to 5.47 trillion roubles, despite high world prices, because of the strong rouble, discounts and damaged refineries. The budget deficit reached 5.19 trillion roubles, about 2.2% of GDP, and the full-year target has been raised to 3.2%. Growth is close to zero: the IMF expects 0.9% this year, the Economy Ministry 0.6%, with the key rate at 14% and inflation at 6.35%.

## Europe

Europe has rejected the American approach. The European Council “regrets” the US decision on diesel, the Commission restated on 19 October that no sanctions will be eased until the war ends, and the EU’s ban on Russian LNG takes full effect at the end of 2026, with pipeline gas to follow in 2027. Yet the EU still bought about €1.4bn of Russian gas in September, and TTF gas prices are up 211% this year as Gulf LNG has been disrupted.

For defence stocks, the waiver cuts both ways. A US step back from pressuring Moscow raises the burden on Europe, which helped Rheinmetall and Leonardo on Friday; but any move towards a settlement lowers the urgency of rearmament, which weighed on them on Monday. The sector has multiplied since February 2022 but most of it is down this year as peace talks resurfaced.

## Politics

The reactions follow predictable lines. In Moscow, envoy Kirill Dmitriev called the waiver “a truly historic moment”. In Kyiv, President Zelensky said it “definitely helps only the Russians”; Bessent, responding to Zelensky’s separate complaint about Starlink, warned him to “be very careful”. The next test is diplomatic: according to the Wall Street Journal, Putin proposed a three-way meeting with Trump and Xi Jinping, without Ukraine, possibly at the APEC summit in Shenzhen on 18 and 19 November. Rubio said such a meeting had been discussed but not finalised.

## Scenarios

We see three paths for mid-December. In our base case the waiver stays in place while talks continue, keeping Russian oil flowing to Asia and leaving the EU to carry the pressure alone. A deal at or after APEC could lead to formal US relief, while a breakdown, or pressure from Congress, could revive the Act’s tariffs. For oil, the Gulf matters more in all three.

**Scenarios for the 60-day review (to mid-December)**

- **Drift: waiver stays, talks continue** (50%): oil neutral; EU sanctions stay, defence range-bound; rouble stable
- **Deal: summit, formal US relief** (25%): oil lower (−$3 to −$5); transatlantic split, defence lower; rouble stronger
- **Reversal: Act tariffs reimposed** (25%): oil higher (+$5 to +$10); defence higher, India tariff risk; rouble weaker

Source: Andra Markets scenarios; probabilities and price effects are judgemental estimates, not forecasts.
