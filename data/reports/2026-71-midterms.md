## Executive Summary

**The base rate favours change.** The president’s party has lost House seats in all but three midterms since 1934, according to Capital Group. This cycle adds inflation and energy prices driven by the Iran war, and a labour market that added only 29,000 jobs in September.

**Most of the House outcome is in the price.** With markets above 90% for a Democratic House, a Republican hold would be the surprise. The Senate carries genuine uncertainty: five battleground races sit within three points in polling averages.

**History is kind to the year after.** A bank study of 31 midterms found the S&P 500 averaged 2.9% in the twelve months before the vote and 12.4% in the twelve after. The pattern says more about uncertainty clearing than about which party wins.

**Trade policy is now executive-led.** After the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs in February, the administration moved to Section 122 and then, from 24 July, to Section 301 tariffs of 10% to 12.5% on 60 economies with no scheduled expiry. Congress has more say over some tools than others.

**Energy and affordability dominate both agendas.** Democrats plan oversight of fuel prices and refiners and legislation to reverse tariffs; Republicans campaign on the tax cuts of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and expanded domestic energy production.

**Our reading.** For markets the result matters through oversight intensity, budget negotiations and the legislative calendar more than through new law, because a Democratic Congress would still need the president’s signature. The Senate result is the open variable.

## The House: largely priced

Republicans enter the election with a narrow House majority, after a cycle that included mid-decade redistricting in several states. Polymarket’s market implies roughly 236 Democratic seats and 52 Democratic Senate seats. The 218-seat majority line sits near the bottom of those ranges.

## Markets and Midterms: what history shows

A bank study of 31 midterms over 125 years, cited by The Berkshire Edge, found the S&P 500 averaged 2.9% in the twelve months before the vote and 12.4% in the twelve months after, against 8.7% across all periods. A Motley Fool analysis finds the index rose in the year after 32 of the last 38 midterms since 1874, by 14% on average, and Schwab’s research shows it was higher six months after the vote in every one of the 13 midterms in its sample. Divided government under a Republican president followed 14 midterms and the market was positive in the next twelve months 93% of the time. A separate analysis cited by Motley Fool found divided government after midterms linked to slightly weaker returns in the first three months, a dip of about 1%.

Our own calculation on monthly S&P 500 data confirms the pattern. Across the 19 midterms since 1950, the index rose in the twelve months after the vote in 18 of 19 cases, by about 15% on average, while the twelve months before the vote gained only modestly on average, with large losses in 1962, 1974, 2002 and 2022. The only year without a gain was 1986, when the index was flat a year later after the October 1987 crash. The 2026 path has been the exception so far: the index rose strongly into the vote, driven by the AI trade, which leaves less of the usual pre-election discount to unwind.

Source: S&P 500 monthly averages (Shiller data via public S&P 500 dataset) to September 2026; Andra Markets calculations. 19 midterms, 1950-2022. Past performance is not indicative of future results.

These averages come with heavy caveats. Samples are small, and this cycle includes an oil shock, a Fed that raised rates in September and a war in the Gulf. Capital Group notes that tariffs, inflation, interest rates and geopolitics weigh at least as much as the election itself.

## The Policy Channels: trade, energy, taxes and oversight

Trade policy has been reshaped by the courts. On 20 February the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that IEEPA does not authorise tariffs. The administration replaced them with a 10% global surcharge under Section 122, which by law lapsed after 150 days on 24 July; the same day, new Section 301 tariffs of 10% to 12.5% on 60 economies took effect, with no scheduled expiry. Section 232 duties on steel, aluminium, copper, autos and pharmaceuticals remain, Section 338 tariffs of 50% on Canada took effect on 19 August, and importers are seeking refunds of an estimated $150 billion or more of IEEPA duties. The US-China tariff truce and related Section 301 exclusions expire on 10 November, a week after the vote.

House Democrats have prepared a 100-days agenda focused on affordability and oversight. According to AP and Bloomberg Government, it includes measures to reverse the administration’s tariffs, an enhanced child tax credit, permanent health-care subsidies and extensive investigations, with possible hearings on gasoline prices, refinery capacity and price gouging. Republicans are campaigning on their record: Senate Majority Leader John Thune frames the message around safety, economics and opportunity, citing the One Big Beautiful Bill Act’s tax cuts, including no tax on tips and permanent 2017 rates, together with border and energy policy and expanded domestic production.

- **Trade**: Democrats (if in majority), legislation to reverse the administration’s tariffs; Republicans (record and plans), keep the administration’s tariff framework.
- **Energy**: Democrats, oversight of fuel prices, refiners and producers; Republicans, expanded domestic production and leasing.
- **Taxes**: Democrats, enhanced child tax credit; Republicans, defend the OBBBA tax cuts.
- **Health**: Democrats, permanent ACA subsidies; Republicans, lower premiums for some, reduced subsidies.
- **Oversight**: Democrats, extensive investigations of the administration; Republicans, n.a.

## The Macro Backdrop: the Fed, inflation and the vote

The election arrives with inflation at 3.7%, energy prices up about 20% from a year earlier and a labour market that added only 29,000 jobs in September. On 28 October the Federal Reserve held rates at 3.75% to 4% in a 10-2 vote, with two officials preferring a hike; Chair Kevin Warsh denied that politics played any role in holding six days before the vote. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent urged the Fed to keep an open mind about the transitory nature of the supply shock. The next Fed decision, on 9 December, will be the first after the new Congress is known. Whatever the result, the incoming Congress inherits an energy-driven inflation problem and a central bank that has signalled it is not yet done.
