**Coverage summary.** In the three weeks since the Federal Reserve raised its target range to 3.75% to 4.00% on 16 September, the research departments and executives of the largest US banks have revised their forecasts and published their views on rates and markets. This note brings those views together. On policy, the range runs from Bank of America, which expects two more increases by December, to Citi, which sees a reasonable chance of a pause in October. On bonds, Bank of America has raised its year-end forecast for the ten-year Treasury yield to 5 per cent, a level the market has already exceeded. On equities, strategists still expect stocks to rise, while warning that bonds have become serious competition. Bank executives, led by Jamie Dimon, are the most cautious voices of all.

## Executive Summary

**Agreement on direction, not on distance.** None of the large banks now expects the Federal Reserve to cut rates this year. The debate is between one more increase, the view of Goldman Sachs, Wells Fargo and current market pricing, and two more, the view of Bank of America and Morgan Stanley. The difference matters: it implies a peak in the policy rate of either 4.25 or 4.50 per cent.

**The data has turned the debate.** A hawkish Fed meeting first pushed forecasts higher, but a softer inflation reading for August and a September jobs report with only 29,000 new jobs, against expectations of about 90,000, led several banks to delay their calls. Goldman Sachs moved its next increase from October to December and now sees a strong chance the Fed decides no more increases are needed.

## Background

At the start of 2026, most banks expected the Federal Reserve to keep cutting rates. That changed during the year as inflation moved back above 3 per cent, driven by tariffs, energy prices linked to the conflict in the Middle East and strong demand related to AI investment. In June, Bank of America became one of the first large banks to forecast increases, and on 16 September the Fed raised rates by 25 basis points, unanimously, to 3.75% to 4.00%. The minutes, published on 7 October and analysed in our note 2026-30, showed that most officials expect another increase by year end, although they disagree on why.

Since the meeting, the data has been mixed. Core PCE inflation for August rose 0.25 per cent on the month, below expectations. Payrolls rose just 29,000 in September and earlier months were revised down by 60,000, while the unemployment rate rose to 4.2 per cent from 4.1 per cent and wage growth slowed to 3.0 per cent a year. Growth, by contrast, remains strong, with Goldman Sachs tracking third-quarter GDP growth of 3.3 per cent.

## The Fed Call, Bank by Bank

Bank of America, in its weekly of 18 September titled “Hiking season in full bloom”, expects increases in October and December, taking the upper bound to 4.50 per cent. Morgan Stanley, which had expected a pause, now forecasts increases in December and March to the same peak, after chief economist Seth Carpenter said the Fed’s view that policy is not restrictive enough was the more important signal. Goldman Sachs called the September meeting “more hawkish than we expected” and added a second increase, but on 30 September moved it from October to December. Wells Fargo expects one more increase this year to 4.00 to 4.25 per cent and none in 2027, arguing that most of the excess inflation comes from supply. Citi’s Andrew Hollenhorst sees the October decision as dependent on September core inflation, saying a reading of 0.2 per cent or less could keep the Fed on hold.

Source: Andra Markets compilation of bank research as reported by Investing.com, Yahoo Finance, Finvaulta and others (17 September to 7 October 2026). Current range 3.75% to 4.00%. Citi’s comments refer to October only.

Behind the forecasts lie two readings of the same inflation. Banks that expect fewer increases, such as Goldman Sachs and Wells Fargo, stress that much of the excess inflation comes from energy and tariffs, which higher rates cannot fix, and that wage growth is moderate. Goldman expects core PCE inflation of 3.0 per cent in the fourth quarter, below the Fed’s own median of 3.4 per cent. Banks that expect more increases, such as Bank of America and Morgan Stanley, point to strong consumer spending, and to the Fed’s own assessment that policy is barely restrictive.

## Bond Yields

The bond market has moved faster than the forecasters. On 24 September Bank of America raised its year-end forecast for the ten-year Treasury yield to 5.0 per cent from 4.5 per cent, citing energy risks related to Iran, fiscal challenges, trade tensions and uncertainty over AI ahead of the midterm elections. By 7 October the yield was already 5.30 per cent and the thirty-year 5.68 per cent. Among European banks, SEB expects 5.10 per cent at the end of the year, while Danske Bank warns of a risk that ten and thirty-year yields reach 6 per cent. Wells Fargo argues that the floor for long-term yields has risen because of solid growth, heavy government and corporate bond issuance and policy uncertainty.

Source: Investing.com (BofA, 24 September 2026); BigGo Finance (SEB, Danske Bank and market levels, 7 October 2026).

## Equity Strategists

Equity strategists remain positive but more cautious than earlier in the year. Goldman Sachs raised its year-end S&P 500 target to 8,000 in May, on earnings growth driven by AI investment. JPMorgan raised its target to 7,800 in June while warning of a high probability of a flash crash in speculative AI stocks. On 7 October, JPMorgan’s strategists led by Mislav Matejka argued that the recent sell-off driven by bond yields looked overdone, that sentiment had become too bearish, and that the unwinding of momentum trades was “a reset” that could allow stocks to rise into year end, with semiconductors as their top pick.

The sharpest warning came from Bank of America. Savita Subramanian, its head of US equity strategy, said its long-term valuation model points to annual S&P 500 returns below 5 per cent over the next decade. “Bonds are becoming a formidable competitor to stocks for the first time in decades,” she wrote, while stressing that this is a ten-year valuation judgement rather than a short-term prediction. With ten-year Treasuries yielding over 5 per cent, investors can now earn a similar long-term return with far less risk. Citi’s equity strategist Scott Chronert struck a different note on 1 October, suggesting the Fed could be in pause mode and that its next move could even be a cut.

## The Banks’ Own Executives

The executives who run the banks have been more cautious than their research departments. Jamie Dimon of JPMorgan said after the September increase that “it’s not clear to me we’ve slayed inflation”, urged companies to prepare for volatile rates, and argued that rising unemployment would be the trigger for credit losses. His co-president Doug Petno said in September that business conditions felt “too good” to bankers who have lived through many cycles. Bank of America, for its part, told investors in mid-September that its investment banking fees would fall about 10 per cent in the third quarter, a guidance that briefly sent bank shares lower.

## Agreement and Disagreement

Three points are shared by almost every bank. First, there will be no rate cuts in 2026 and the policy rate will peak between 4.25 and 4.50 per cent. Second, inflation will stay above 3 per cent into 2027 because of energy, tariffs and AI-related demand. Third, long-term yields will stay high, because heavy borrowing by governments and by companies financing AI investment keeps the supply of bonds large.

The disagreement is about the cause of inflation and therefore the cure. If it is mainly supply, from energy and tariffs, more rate increases would slow the economy without fixing prices, and the Fed should stop soon; this is broadly the view of Goldman Sachs, Wells Fargo and Citi. If it is demand, from strong consumers and the AI boom, the Fed needs to go further; this is the view of Bank of America and Morgan Stanley. The same split runs through the Federal Reserve itself, as the September minutes showed. The September jobs report has tilted the argument towards the first camp, at least for October.

## Market Implications

For bond investors, the consensus implies that short-term rates have little room to fall and that long-term yields, already above forecasts, could keep rising if inflation does not slow. For equity investors, high yields raise the bar: stocks need strong earnings growth to keep up, which favours companies benefiting from AI investment and penalises those with high debt. Banks such as JPMorgan benefit from higher rates through their net interest income as long as unemployment does not rise.

## Outlook and Monitoring Points

The next two weeks will test the forecasts. The September consumer price index, which Citi sees as the decisive figure for October, the start of bank earnings on 13 October, where executives will update their views on the economy and credit, and the October jobs report will shape the Fed’s decision on 28 October. Investors should watch whether ten-year yields hold above 5 per cent, whether more banks move their calls from October to December, and whether any bank returns to forecasting rate cuts in 2027. For now, Wall Street agrees that rates will stay high; the argument is only about how high.
