## Executive Summary

**Earnings have beaten rates so far.** Higher bond yields normally hurt highly valued growth stocks, and in 2022 they sent the SOX down 36 per cent. This year, the opposite has happened: earnings have grown so fast that they have more than offset the rise in yields. On our calculation, the daily link between chip stocks and real yields in 2026 is negative but weak.

**The risk is a combination, not rates alone.** One more 25 basis point increase in December, which markets already price, should matter little on its own. The real risk is a scenario in which yields keep rising towards 6 per cent, raising the cost of the debt that funds AI data centres, at the same time as investors start to doubt the returns on that spending. That combination drove both sell-offs this year.

**Not all chip stocks are equally exposed.** Companies on high multiples, such as AMD at 58 times forward earnings, and the most cyclical businesses, equipment makers and memory, would suffer most. Nvidia, TSMC and Broadcom, on about 20 to 22 times earnings with visible demand into 2027, are better protected.

## The State of the Market

The SOX more than doubled in the first half, reaching a record of 14,635 on 22 June. In July, the “AI trade” unwound: the index lost about 10 per cent in a single week and fell 29 per cent from its high by 29 July, amid profit-taking, doubts about AI spending, a strong Chinese open-source AI model and high leverage among investors. It recovered in August, then fell again in mid-September, losing 5.9 per cent on 14 September as the ten-year yield passed 5 per cent and a Fed increase became almost certain. Since then it has rebounded 17 per cent and stands about 11 per cent below its high.

## The Demand Engine

The scale of the upswing is unprecedented. According to the Semiconductor Industry Association, global chip sales reached US$146.8 billion in July on a three-month average, 135 per cent more than a year earlier. The industry body WSTS forecasts the market to grow about 90 per cent in 2026 to about US$1.5 trillion, with memory sales up about 250 per cent as prices soar, and a further 27 per cent in 2027 to about US$1.9 trillion. Behind this is the investment of the large cloud companies: Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta together plan to spend about US$720 to 745 billion this year on our calculation, and Moody’s expects the total to approach US$1 trillion in 2027.

## Valuation

Despite the rally, sector valuations are not extreme, because earnings have risen almost as fast as prices. In July, the SOXX exchange-traded fund traded at 22.8 times forward earnings against 20.3 times for the S&P 500, according to LSEG data, while Standard Chartered estimated in September that semiconductors traded at about 16 times, below their five-year average of about 20. The difference depends on which companies are included and on how quickly analysts have raised forecasts. For comparison, the SOX traded at 18.2 times at the bottom of the 2022 bear market, and memory makers such as Micron and SK hynix trade at single-digit multiples because investors expect their profits to fall when the cycle turns.

## How Rate Increases Affect Chip Stocks

Higher interest rates can affect the sector through four channels. The first is valuation: a higher risk-free rate lowers the present value of profits far in the future, which hurts growth stocks the most. The second, specific to this cycle, is financing: the large cloud companies issued about US$170 billion of bonds in the first half of 2026 to fund data centres, Meta’s long bonds trade in line with lower-rated BBB credits, and Oracle is raising about US$40 billion of debt and equity; higher rates make each new data centre more expensive. The third is demand from consumers and industry, since higher borrowing costs weigh on purchases of phones, PCs and cars. The fourth is the dollar, which tends to strengthen when the Fed tightens and reduces the value of foreign sales for US companies.

History shows how damaging the combination can be. In 2018, when the Fed raised rates four times, the SOX fell 8 per cent over the year and 26 per cent from peak to trough. In 2022, when the Fed raised rates by more than four percentage points and the ten-year real yield rose from minus 1 to plus 1.6 per cent, the index lost 36 per cent, and analysts cut their earnings forecasts by 17 per cent. The difference in 2026 is that earnings are rising, not falling. As long as that continues, higher rates slow the rally rather than reverse it.

A simple calculation shows why valuation matters in the hawkish case. If higher yields led investors to pay three fewer times earnings for a company on 20 times, such as Nvidia or TSMC, its shares would fall about 15 per cent with unchanged forecasts; if AMD’s multiple fell from 58 to 45 times, its shares would fall about 22 per cent. These figures are illustrative, but they show that the most expensive stocks have the most to lose if rates keep rising, while strong earnings growth can absorb part of the blow, as it has this year.

## Risk Considerations

Rates are only one of the risks. The largest is the AI investment cycle itself: chip demand depends on a few very large buyers whose spending is now partly financed with debt, and a slowdown would hit equipment makers and memory first. Memory prices, which have driven much of this year’s growth, could fall as quickly as they rose once new capacity arrives. US tariffs on imported chips may be extended to servers and devices, export controls limit sales to China, and tensions around Taiwan remain a risk for the whole supply chain.

As long as earnings keep rising faster than yields, the sector can live with a tighter Fed.
