**Coverage summary.** The University of Michigan’s final October reading came in at 48.0, the second-lowest on record, with current conditions falling to 46.7 from 50.9 and one-year inflation expectations rising to 4.7%. The Conference Board’s measure rebounded to 91.7, its strongest monthly gain since March 2021, as gasoline eased from September. Underneath the surveys the fundamentals are softer: payrolls rose only 29,000 in September, unemployment edged up to 4.2% and average hourly earnings grew 3.0% against inflation of 3.7%. Even so, the National Retail Federation expects holiday sales to grow 4.2% to $1.013 trillion. The question for the next two months is how long a split consumer can keep spending.

## Executive Summary

**Energy is doing the damage.** Michigan attributed the spring collapse in sentiment to Hormuz-driven gasoline prices, which peaked at $4.56 a gallon in May, the highest since July 2022. Lower-income households, for whom fuel is a larger share of the budget, move most when pump prices change.

**Real incomes are now shrinking.** Average hourly earnings rose 3.0% over the year to September while consumer prices rose 3.7%. By our calculation real hourly pay is falling by about 0.7%, which leaves savings and credit to fill the gap.

**The labour market has stalled.** Payrolls rose 29,000 in September against a forecast of 84,000, July was revised to a loss and the twelve-month average has fallen to 45,000, below the 50,000 to 80,000 economists estimate is needed to absorb labour-force growth.

**The split is by income.** Executives at McDonald’s, Kraft Heinz, Procter & Gamble and Albertsons describe the same pattern: higher-income customers spend normally, lower-income ones trade down, buy smaller packs and visit less often.

**Retail is still growing.** NRF’s September Retail Monitor showed twelve straight months of growth, and the trade group argues that confidence is a poor predictor of spending. About half of its 4.2% holiday forecast is price, so real growth is roughly 2%, carried by wealthier households.

**Our reading.** The consumer is not breaking, but the cushions are thinning: falling real wages, a weak jobs print, card balances near records and a Fed that held rates at 3.75% to 4% on 28 October with two officials voting for a hike, leaving December open. A further oil spike or a turn in hiring would expose the lower half of the distribution first.

## Sentiment

Michigan sentiment collapsed in the spring as the war pushed fuel prices up, reaching a record low of 44.8 in May. It recovered briefly in June when gasoline eased, then slipped again. The October final reading was revised up from a preliminary 46.3 and beat every forecast in Bloomberg’s survey, but the detail is weaker than the headline: current conditions fell sharply while expectations improved to 48.8, the highest since August. The Conference Board index, which leans more on the labour market, rose 7.5 points to 91.7, helped by lower pump prices; its economists noted that write-in comments about prices, groceries and fuel increased, and that the election ranked below prices and inflation as a concern.

## Inflation and Real Incomes

Expectations matter for policy as much as the mood itself. Michigan’s one-year measure has stayed between 4.6% and 4.8% since May and the long-run measure has drifted back up to 3.5%; the Conference Board’s twelve-month expectation is 5.8%. Actual inflation is not far behind: the CPI rose 0.6% in September alone and 3.7% over the year, with energy up 20.4%. Wages have not kept pace. Average hourly earnings rose 3.0% over the year, and the average workweek held at 34.4 hours.

Sources: BLS via Yahoo Finance and Trading Economics; BLS CPI, 14 Oct 2026; University of Michigan Surveys of Consumers via Reuters/Hirunews, TheStreet and Bloomberg. Real wage estimate is an Andra Markets calculation.

## The Labour Market

So far households have been hit through prices rather than jobs, but that is changing. September payrolls rose 29,000, far below the 84,000 consensus, and revisions cut 60,000 from the two previous months, turning July into a loss of 10,000. The unemployment rate rose to 4.2%, partly because more people started looking for work; retirements and tighter immigration enforcement are keeping it low by shrinking labour supply. Financial activities have shed 129,000 jobs since May 2025, while health care continues to add. The broader U-6 measure is close to 8%. The October report is due on 6 November.

Sources: BLS via CNBC, Yahoo Finance and Kalkine, 2 Oct 2026. Breakeven range is the economists’ estimate cited by Kalkine.

## Credit and Savings

Credit card balances rose $21 billion in the second quarter to $1.263 trillion, up $54 billion from a year earlier and just below the $1.28 trillion record set at the end of 2025, according to the New York Fed. Auto loans reached a record $1.71 trillion. The share of card balances more than 90 days delinquent rose to 12.9% from 12.2% a year earlier, a level last seen around the Great Recession, although the New York Fed argues that much of the rise reflects stale, charged-off debt that lenders report for longer, and that the flow into new delinquency has been broadly stable for two years. Bloomberg reported that the saving rate fell in March to its lowest in three years.

Source: New York Fed Quarterly Report on Household Debt and Credit.

## What Companies Are Saying

Earnings calls this year have told a consistent story. McDonald’s chief executive Chris Kempczinski said elevated gasoline prices disproportionately hurt low-income consumers and that the pressure would continue, while higher-income customers keep spending. Kraft Heinz’s chief executive said some consumers are running out of money at the end of the month. Whirlpool’s chief executive compared a 15% hit to industry demand to the global financial crisis. Procter & Gamble’s finance chief said more pressured consumers are buying smaller pack sizes, and Albertsons reported trade-down to cheaper proteins. Fast food, which skews towards lower incomes, has been hit first.

- **McDonald’s** (Q1 call, May): gas prices hit low-income customers hardest; higher-income spending resilient.
- **Kraft Heinz** (May): some households run out of money at month-end.
- **Whirlpool** (May): ~15% hit to industry demand, likened to the GFC.
- **Procter & Gamble** (Q4 call, July): pressured consumers buy smaller packs; affluent unaffected.
- **Albertsons** (July): trade-down to value packs and cheaper proteins.
- **Domino’s** (April): orders fell as gas prices spiked; more discounts.

Sources: The Consumer Daily; Shopifreaks; PYMNTS; Marketplace; 24/7 Wall St. Comments are paraphrased.

## The Holiday Season

NRF forecasts November and December sales of $1.013 trillion, up 4.2% from $972 billion, the fastest pace since 2021 and the first season above a trillion. Its chief economist, Mark Mathews, said nearly half of that growth should be real and the rest price, with diesel and memory-chip costs pushing up categories such as electronics. Holiday sales account for about 19% of annual retail sales. NRF’s record is a caution: last year it forecast 3.7% to 4.2% and sales grew 3.1%. A synthesis of industry outlooks published in July by The Grayson Company pointed to 2.5% to 3.5%. NRF also expects muted seasonal hiring, which it links to fuel prices, inflation, rates and consumer debt.

Sources: National Retail Federation, 15 Oct 2026; The Grayson Company, July 2026; BLS, 14 Oct 2026. Holiday sales exclude autos, gasoline stations and restaurants.

## The Fed’s Dilemma

The Federal Reserve kept the federal funds range at 3.75% to 4% on 28 October in a 10-2 vote, with Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack and Governor Lisa Cook voting for a 25 basis-point hike. The statement repeated that inflation remains elevated and that more information will help determine the policy stance; Chair Kevin Warsh declined to give forward guidance and called the coming weeks data-rich. The next decision, with new projections, is on 9 December. For households this means borrowing costs stay high into the holidays: mortgage rates are close to ~7% and card rates are near records. A weak October jobs report on 6 November would strengthen the case for waiting; a hot CPI print would do the opposite.
