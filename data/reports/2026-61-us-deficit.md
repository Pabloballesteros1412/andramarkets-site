**Coverage summary.** The September Treasury statement, released on 16 October, confirmed a fiscal 2026 deficit of $1,992.5bn, up 12% on the year and the third largest on record. Unlike 2009 or 2020, it came without a recession; the Iran war has cost about $38bn so far according to CBO, a small part of the total. Net interest reached $1,051.8bn, more than the $965.2bn spent on national defence, as the average rate on Treasury debt rose to 3.5% and long-term yields climbed above 5%. Moody’s Analytics’ chief economist Mark Zandi now warns of a “fiscal reckoning”.

## Executive Summary

**A crisis-size deficit without a crisis.** At 6.2% of GDP with unemployment low, the deficit is far above what the economic cycle would explain, and war costs account for only a small part of it. Revenue grew only 3%: corporate taxes fell after the 2025 tax law and customs receipts dropped as the Supreme Court struck down the emergency tariffs and refunds were paid.

**Interest is now the fastest-growing item.** Net interest has tripled since 2020 and absorbs almost a fifth of federal revenue. The problem compounds: CBO estimates that rates 1.5 points above its baseline would add $6tn to deficits over ten years.

**The bond market is charging more.** The ten-year yield closed at 5.31% on 5 October, the highest since 2002, and the term premium, the extra return investors demand for holding long bonds, is the highest since 2010. Mortgage rates are at 7.4%.

**But demand is holding.** Recent ten- and thirty-year auctions were well covered and foreign holdings rose to $9.33tn in August. This is a pricing problem, not yet a funding problem.

## The Deficit

The deficit has stayed between $1.7tn and $2.0tn for four years despite full employment. In fiscal 2026 outlays rose 5.5% to $7.4tn while receipts rose only 3.2% to $5.4tn. September, usually a surplus month thanks to quarterly tax payments, ended $27bn in deficit. CBO had projected about $1.9tn in February; the outturn was about $135bn worse, according to the CRFB.

Two policy changes explain much of the revenue gap. The 2025 tax law cut business taxes, and corporate receipts fell 15.6%. Tariffs were meant to compensate, but after the Supreme Court ruled in February that emergency powers do not authorise tariffs, about $145bn was refunded during the year and customs duties fell 11% to $173bn. Spending, meanwhile, is driven by ageing and interest: Social Security, Medicare and net interest together grew by about $245bn.

## The Cost of Interest

Net interest has risen from $345bn in fiscal 2020 to $1,052bn, as the debt grew and the cheap pandemic-era borrowing was refinanced at higher rates. The average rate on marketable Treasury debt was 3.52% at the end of September, with bills at 3.87%; every year more old debt rolls over into today’s higher yields. Interest now exceeds spending on defence and is close to Medicare.

CBO projects net interest of about $2.1tn, or 4.6% of GDP, by 2036 under its February baseline. Its new scenarios, published on 8 October, show the sensitivity: if rates run 1.5 points above baseline, deficits are $6tn larger over ten years and debt reaches 133% of GDP, against 120% in the baseline.

## The Debt

Total public debt passed $40tn on 18 August and stood at $40.4tn on 16 October, of which $32.5tn is held by the public. CBO expects debt held by the public to reach about 101% of GDP this year and, according to the CRFB’s reading of its projections, to pass the post-war record of 106% around 2030. The statutory debt limit, raised by $5tn in July 2025 to $41.1tn, is likely to bind again in 2027.

Source: Treasury and BEA via FRED (FYGFGDQ188S), quarterly to Q1 2026, when federal debt held by the public stood at 98.7% of GDP.

## The Bond Market

Long yields rose by as much as 1.1 points this year. The ten-year closed at 5.31% on 5 October and the thirty-year at 5.67% on 7 October, both the highest since 2002, after what Reuters called the worst quarter for Treasuries since 1994. Part of the move reflects inflation and the Fed’s September hike, but a growing share is term premium: compensation for holding long-dated debt amid fiscal and inflation uncertainty.

Sources: Federal Reserve H.15 via FRED (DGS10), daily closes sampled weekly, to 16 October 2026, when the ten-year yield was 5.09%. Federal Reserve Board (Kim-Wright) via FRED (THREEFYTP10), with the ten-year term premium at 0.99%. The NY Fed ACM measure peaked near 0.98% on 7 October and was about 0.75% by 15 October.

The rise passes straight to households. The 30-year mortgage rate was 7.40% on 15 October, against 6.27% a year earlier, and housing starts fell 5.2% in September; the home builders’ sentiment index is at its lowest since 2012.

## Supply and Demand

Despite the headlines, demand at auction has been solid. The 7 October ten-year auction sold $39bn at 5.300%, the highest auction yield since 2000, with strong bidding; the thirty-year a day later was in line. The weak spots were the twenty-year in September and a thirty-year in August. Foreign holdings rose by $83bn to $9.33tn in August, but the mix is shifting: China’s holdings are the lowest since 2008, the UK’s passed $1tn for the first time.

The supply outlook is the next test. Treasury kept coupon sizes unchanged in August and has leaned on bills and buybacks, but its advisory committee warned that projections “could warrant increases in coupon issuance in FY27”. The next refunding announcement is on 4 November, the day after the midterm elections.

## Ratings and Investors

All three major agencies now rate the US AA+ or Aa1. Moody’s downgraded in May 2025; S&P affirmed AA+ with a stable outlook in June but said it could downgrade within two years if deficits exceed expectations; Fitch affirmed in August. Investors are wary: in BofA’s October survey, short Treasuries was the second most crowded trade and fund managers said they would only switch from stocks to bonds at an average ten-year yield of 5.9%.

## Policy Options

There is no deficit-reduction plan in Congress. The government is funded only until 11 December by a stopgap, the midterm elections on 3 November could change control of one or both chambers, and the debt limit returns in 2027. The options are familiar: higher revenue, slower growth in entitlements, or tolerating higher inflation. Penn Wharton’s Kent Smetters expects the last.

**Scenarios for long-term yields over the next 12 months**

- **Contained: Fed holds, deficit stabilises** (40%): 10-year 4.6% to 5.0%; trigger: softer oil and inflation; demand at auctions
- **Grind higher: no fiscal plan, Fed hikes in Dec** (40%): 10-year 5.2% to 5.6%; trigger: larger coupon issuance; term premium rises
- **Disorderly: buyers strike** (20%): 10-year above 6%; trigger: failed auction, downgrade or debt-limit standoff
