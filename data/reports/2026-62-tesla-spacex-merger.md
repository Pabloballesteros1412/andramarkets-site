## Executive Summary

**A process, not a deal.** Forming independent committees is the procedural step that Tesla skipped in its 2016 purchase of SolarCity, which led to years of litigation. It does not commit either board to a transaction, and Musk himself called it “an odd kabuki dance”. Still, he said plainly that he thinks a merger would be “a good idea”.

**SpaceX has become an AI company.** After absorbing xAI in February, its “Super Intelligence” segment generated $7.6bn of the $14.6bn of third-quarter revenue, more than Starlink. The strategic case rests on putting Tesla’s robots, cars and chips together with SpaceX’s models and compute.

**The hard part is the price.** Tesla trades on a premium for autonomy and robots, with operating margins near zero in the second quarter; SpaceX trades at about 34 times annualised revenue. Every exchange ratio transfers value between two sets of shareholders, and Dan Ives argues Tesla would need to trade near $550 for a deal to win approval.

**Our view.** We assign a 35% probability to agreed terms within twelve months, with protracted talks the most likely single outcome. The near-term catalyst is Tesla’s results tonight.

## The Announcement

The disclosure came almost in passing on the SpaceX call. Musk described himself as a conflicted party who would not take part in negotiations, said that putting a price on any deal would come “much further down the road, if at all”, and warned that calling a merger certain “would be overstating it”. Tesla’s independent directors include chair Robyn Denholm, James Murdoch and JB Straubel; SpaceX’s include Steve Jurvetson, Luke Nosek and Roelof Botha. Ira Ehrenpreis sits on both boards, a detail that will draw scrutiny. Neither company has named financial or legal advisers.

Since the SpaceX listing both stocks have lagged the market. SpaceX closed at a record $211 two sessions after its debut, fell to $108 in early August and closed at $147.79 on 20 October, 9% above its $135 offer price. Tesla is down 17% this year. The merger news therefore arrives with both shares well below their highs, which matters for how any ratio is set.

## SpaceX After the IPO

SpaceX listed on Nasdaq in June after raising $75bn at $135 a share ($85.7bn including the over-allotment), the largest IPO ever, at a valuation of about $1.8tn. In February it had acquired xAI in a deal valuing the two at $1.25tn. Revenue has accelerated each quarter since, from $4.7bn in the first quarter to $7.8bn in the second and $14.6bn in the third, when it also earned $4.4bn of net income. Management guides to $33bn to $37bn of revenue in the first quarter of 2027 and repeated its target of $100bn of annualised revenue by the end of 2026.

The growth is expensive. SpaceX spent $19.6bn on capital investment in the third quarter and expects $35bn to $40bn in the fourth, mostly on AI compute. Starlink remains the profitable core: it reached 13.6 million subscribers, up from 8.9 million at the end of 2025, and the connectivity segment earned a 63% EBITDA margin last year. Launch revenue was $1.8bn in the quarter.

At current prices one Tesla share is worth about 2.5 SpaceX shares. Any ratio below that hands value to SpaceX holders; any premium above it rewards Tesla holders. Because Musk owns roughly twice as much of SpaceX as of Tesla, he would gain from a ratio that favours SpaceX, which is precisely the conflict the committees are meant to manage. On Fortune’s numbers he would own about a third of the combined group; analysts quoted by Reuters estimate his voting power could reach 57% to 58% under one illustrative structure.

## Governance

The SolarCity precedent shapes the process. Tesla bought the solar installer, which Musk chaired and his cousins ran, in 2016 without an independent committee; shareholders sued, and the Delaware Supreme Court upheld the deal only in 2023, noting that such a committee is best practice though not required. Tesla has since moved its incorporation to Texas, where shareholder suits are harder to bring. The approval threshold is the real hurdle: a majority of all outstanding shares, so retail holders who do not vote count against.

**Governance hurdles**

- **Musk on both sides**: controls SpaceX, ~20% of Tesla; gains from a SpaceX-friendly ratio
- **Shared director**: Ira Ehrenpreis sits on both boards
- **Related-party links**: Tesla holds SpaceX shares via its $2bn xAI investment; SpaceX director’s fund Valor holds 3.4% of SpaceX
- **Approval threshold**: majority of all outstanding Tesla shares
- **Pay package**: $1tn award; terms on a change of control may be contested
- **Lock-ups**: Musk’s SpaceX lock-up to mid-2027; restricted Tesla shares to Jan 2028

Sources: TechCrunch; Electrek; Reuters via BNN Bloomberg; SEC filings; Inc.

The reaction also says something about the wider AI trade. The same week, Citadel’s Ken Griffin said AI is “almost certainly” in a bubble while calling the technology transformative, and credit investors are pricing more risk in technology debt as spending on data centres rises. A combined Tesla and SpaceX would be the largest single bet on that spending, with combined capital spending on track to exceed $100bn this year on current guidance. For investors, the merger question is also a question about how much AI exposure they want in one stock.
