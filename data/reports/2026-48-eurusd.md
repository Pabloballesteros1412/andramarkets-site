Three forces explain most of the decline: a Federal Reserve that has returned to raising rates faster than the European Central Bank, an energy shock linked to the conflict with Iran that hurts the euro area more than the United States, and renewed fiscal stress in France. ## Executive Summary

**Policy divergence has widened.** Both central banks are raising rates, but the Fed is ahead: its range of 3.75 to 4.00 per cent compares with the ECB’s 2.50 per cent deposit rate, and the two-year yield gap between the United States and the euro area has widened by about 41 basis points this year on our measure. Our regression links each percentage point of that spread to about 10.7 cents on EUR/USD over two years.

**Energy is the second driver.** WTI crude has risen from about US$57 at the end of 2025 to more than US$90. In 2026, the rolling 26-week correlation between EUR/USD and oil reached −0.65, far stronger than its average of about −0.25 in 2024 and 2025. Higher oil prices worsen the euro area’s terms of trade and lift its inflation without improving its growth.

**Fiscal risk has returned.** The spread between French and German ten-year yields reached about 140 to 152 basis points in the week to 2 October, the widest since 2011-12, as France presented a 2027 budget with a deficit target of 5.0 per cent of GDP. A snap election in Spain on 29 November adds to the political calendar.

**Valuation is no longer stretched.** Depending on the window and the model, our rate-based fair value ranges from about 1.10 to 1.14, against a spot of 1.1186; the euro is cheap relative to its one-year history (z-score −2.9, RSI 27), but on our data such readings have not reliably produced rebounds. We expect a range of 1.10 to 1.14, with downside risks until the French budget vote and the 28 to 29 October central bank meetings.

## The Year So Far

The euro started 2026 near 1.175 and rose to 1.1974 on 28 January, its highest level since June 2021, extending the 13 per cent rally of 2025. The turning point came on 28 February, when US and Israeli strikes on Iran began; with Brent above US$100 in early March, EUR/USD fell to about 1.153 and option markets turned to protect against further euro weakness. A ceasefire in April lifted the euro back to 1.173, and the largest daily gain of the year (+1.28 per cent) came on 8 April. From June, the drivers turned negative again: the ECB’s first increase on 11 June was outweighed by a hawkish Fed, producing the largest daily fall of the year (−1.13 per cent) on 18 June. After a recovery in August, the euro fell for four consecutive weeks from mid-September, as the Fed raised rates, euro area inflation surprised higher and French bond spreads widened.

## Monetary Policy Divergence

The ECB raised its deposit rate on 11 June, its first increase since 2023, and again on 10 September, to 2.50 per cent. Its September projections show inflation of 3.0 per cent this year and 2.5 per cent in 2027, revised up, with growth of 0.9 per cent. Euro area inflation then rose to 3.8 per cent in September, with energy prices up almost 19 per cent on a year earlier. Yet President Lagarde’s remarks in late September were read as dovish, setting a high bar for an increase on 29 October, according to Ebury. Markets price the deposit rate at about 2.72 per cent by December. In the United States, the Fed raised its range to 3.75 to 4.00 per cent on 16 September, Chair Kevin Warsh said that inflation “is too high and has been for too long”, and the minutes published on 7 October show that most officials expect another increase this year.

The relationship between the euro and the yield spread is clear over longer windows. Using weekly data over two years, a one-point increase in the spread is associated with a fall of about 10.7 cents in EUR/USD, and the spread explains about half of the variation in the exchange rate. Over the past year the link has been weaker on a week-to-week basis, because energy and political risk have played a larger role, but the direction is consistent: the spread widened from 1.36 points at the end of 2025 to 1.77 points on 8 October, on the ECB’s euro area curve.

## Fair Value

Five simple models place fair value between 1.106 and 1.142, with spot at 1.1186 in the middle. The models that use only the past year suggest the euro is about 2 per cent cheap, because during most of that year the euro traded above what rates alone justified; longer windows and models that include oil suggest it is roughly fairly valued. The conclusion is that the recent fall has removed the euro’s earlier premium rather than created a large discount. On longer-term measures, purchasing-power parity estimates are well above the current rate (BNP Paribas cited about 1.26 to 1.41 in 2025, an older estimate), which matters for multi-year investors but rarely for the next few months.

## Energy and Terms of Trade

The euro area imports most of its energy, so higher oil and gas prices transfer income abroad, weaken its trade balance and raise inflation in a way that monetary policy cannot fully offset. The ECB’s account of its September meeting noted oil at about US$97 and gas at about €73 per megawatt hour. On FRED data, WTI rose from US$57 at the end of 2025 to about US$96 in early October. The rolling correlation between weekly changes in EUR/USD and WTI shows that the euro’s sensitivity to oil peaked in 2026; this is why the April ceasefire produced the euro’s best day of the year, why oil-easing news such as President Trump’s statement that the US would not attack Iran before the midterm elections tends to help it, and why escalation remains its main external risk.

## Fiscal and Political Risk

France presented a 2027 budget on 1 October with about €54 billion of savings and extra revenue, a deficit of 5.4 per cent of GDP this year and a target of 5.0 per cent in 2027, and public debt of 119 per cent of GDP. Its minority government faces parliamentary debate from 13 October, with a presidential election due in spring 2027. In the week to 2 October, the ten-year French yield reached 5 per cent and the spread over Germany widened by about 34 basis points, the largest weekly increase in 17 years according to Reuters. France now yields more than Italy, whose spread is about 107 basis points. Spain’s prime minister called a snap election for 29 November. Germany, by contrast, is expanding spending: its 2027 draft budget includes €117.5 billion of investment and defence spending rising towards 3.5 per cent of GDP by 2029, which supports euro area growth but also adds to bond supply. For the euro, French spreads have become a second interest-rate differential to watch.

## Volatility and Options

Realised volatility is moderate: 5.2 per cent over one month, in the 24th percentile of the past ten years, and 4.8 per cent over three months. Option markets price more: three-month implied volatility was 6.45 per cent on 1 October, its highest since April, according to Reuters data, and one-month 25-delta risk reversals were −1.24 on 7 October, around the 10th percentile of the past five years according to FXStreet. In other words, investors are paying more for protection against a weaker euro than against a stronger one. The distribution of daily changes over the past year is close to normal (skew +0.24, excess kurtosis 1.2), with a one-day 95 per cent value at risk of −0.59 per cent.

## Flows and Positioning

Speculative positioning has turned sharply: from a net long of about 180,000 contracts in February, CFTC data show a net short of about 63,000 contracts in the week to 29 September according to FXStreet, in the 3.5th percentile of the past five years (other sources, using a different definition, report about 39,000). Short-term positioning therefore already reflects much of the bearish news. Longer-term flows are more supportive. The euro area runs a current account surplus of €282 billion over twelve months (1.7 per cent of GDP), while the United States had a deficit of US$246 billion in the second quarter alone (3.0 per cent of GDP). Central banks also continued to add euros: the IMF reports that the euro’s share of global reserves rose to 20.6 per cent in the second quarter, driven by active purchases.

## Quantitative Signals

On standard quantitative measures, the euro is stretched to the downside: it is 2.8 per cent below its 50-day average and 3.6 per cent below its 200-day, with a z-score of −2.9 and an RSI of 27. Mean reversion would suggest a bounce, but the historical evidence is weak: on our data since 2014, after the 15 non-overlapping months that began with a z-score below −2, EUR/USD fell by 0.2 per cent on average over the following month and rose in only 60 per cent of cases. Carry continues to favour the dollar by about 1.7 points on two-year yields, and the fourth-quarter seasonal pattern is neutral (up in six of the last twelve years).

## Scenarios

The base case keeps the euro within its fair-value band. The two alternative scenarios are not symmetrical: a negative shock from France or from energy would come with wider spreads and higher volatility, and could move the pair quickly, while a positive scenario depends on a sequence of data and decisions over several weeks. Positioning partly offsets this asymmetry, because a market that is already short the euro can react strongly to good news.
