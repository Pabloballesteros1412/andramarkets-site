## Executive Summary

**The trend has turned down.** The pair is 1.8 per cent below its 50-day average and 1.7 per cent below its 200-day, with an RSI of 36, weak but not oversold. It sits in the lower fifth of its 52-week range, about 1.2 per cent above the November 2025 low of 1.3044.

**Volatility is unusually low.** At 4.9 per cent, one-month volatility is about 2.7 points below its ten-year median. Such calm leaves little room for error before a major fiscal event: the last fiscal shock, in the autumn of 2022, pushed one-month volatility above 20 per cent on our data.

**No carry to hold the pound.** Two-year gilts and Treasuries yield almost the same, so investors are not paid to own sterling. Markets price more than 100 basis points of Bank of England increases by the end of 2027; if the Bank delivers less, as MUFG expects, the pound loses support.

## Price and Trend

The pound peaked at 1.3817 on 29 January and has traded between about 1.32 and 1.37 since. The decline of the last month took it below the 50-day average (1.3447) and the 200-day (1.3442), which now sit almost on top of each other, a sign that the medium-term trend has flattened. The z-score against the one-year average is −1.3, so the pound is cheap relative to its recent history but not extremely so.

## Volatility

One-month realised volatility of 4.9 per cent compares with a ten-year median of 7.6 per cent; three-month volatility is similar at 4.8 per cent. The pound has become one of the quietest major currencies, even though UK government bonds have been among the most volatile. That gap is unusual: in past episodes of gilt stress, sterling volatility rose quickly.

## Risk Considerations and Calendar

The main risk is the Budget on 28 October. A plan seen as credible could lower gilt yields and support the pound, while a loss of confidence would push yields and the currency in opposite directions, as in 2022. A weaker US labour market would help sterling through a softer dollar. Key dates: UK CPI on 21 October, the Budget and the FOMC on 28 October, and the Bank of England on 5 November. Low volatility is the statistic to challenge most in the coming weeks.
