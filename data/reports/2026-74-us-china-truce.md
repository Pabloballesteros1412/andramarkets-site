**Coverage summary.** The arrangement struck in Kuala Lumpur and Busan in October 2025 suspended China’s October 2025 rare-earth export controls, the US Entity List affiliates rule and US port fees on Chinese ships for one year, until 10 November 2026. On 23 September Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said it would be extended by two months, to 10 January 2027, and on 28 September China’s commerce ministry confirmed an extension of the “joint arrangement”. But as of 29 October neither government had published the legal notices that carry their own suspensions past 9 and 10 November. Unless they appear, China’s 0.1% extraterritorial rare-earth rule returns on 10 November and the US affiliates rule on the same day. The truce is extended in politics; on paper it still ends in eleven days.

## What the truce covers and when each part ends

- **MOFCOM No. 70** (China; suspends Nos. 55-58, 61, 62): pauses Oct 2025 rare-earth, battery and technology controls, incl. 0.1% rule; legal end 10 Nov 2026; no extension published.
- **MOFCOM No. 72** (China): pauses US-specific dual-use licensing; legal end 27 Nov 2026; no extension published.
- **Affiliates rule suspension** (US, BIS): pauses Entity List rule for majority-owned subsidiaries (~20,000 firms); legal end 9 Nov 2026; no Federal Register notice.
- **Port fees on Chinese ships** (US, USTR; Section 301): pauses maritime and shipbuilding fees; legal end 9-10 Nov 2026; no notice.
- **Fentanyl tariff cut 20% → 10%** (US): voided with IEEPA by the Supreme Court in February; legal end n.a.; replaced by Section 301 layers.
- **Soybean purchases** (China; 25 MMT a year, 2026-28): farm purchase commitment; annual; ~half of 2026 done by Sep.
- **Truce as a whole** (both): political arrangement; ends 10 Jan 2027; announced, not codified.

Sources: MOFCOM via Technology.org and Mainrich International; Federal Register via NAMI; China Briefing; Supply Chain Dive; Bytes Europe (30 Oct 2026); CNBC; White House fact sheets.

## Executive Summary

**The real deadline is a paperwork deadline.** Both governments say the truce runs to 10 January. Neither has yet published the notices that would carry its own suspensions past 9 and 10 November. The first test is whether a Federal Register notice on the affiliates rule appears before 9 November and a MOFCOM announcement before 10 November.

**The extensions are getting shorter.** Busan bought a year; Washington bought two months, with a White House target to block Chinese rare-earth imports and a Pentagon magnet cutoff both falling in January 2027. The truce now ends exactly where the next round of US restrictions begins.

**Tariffs are lower than at the peak, not low.** After the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs in February, China-specific Section 301 duties and a new 12.5% forced-labour tariff left most Chinese goods facing about 20% to 40%. PIIE puts the average effective rate at 24% this year, against 6% for everyone else.

**Trade has decoupled, partly.** China’s share of US imports fell from 21.6% in 2017 to about 9% in 2025 and the bilateral deficit has roughly halved. But China reported $100 billion more exports to the US in January-July than the US recorded as imports, which points to transshipment and under-declaration.

**Rare earths are China’s leverage, and it has not let go.** China controls an estimated 90% of rare-earth processing and magnet capacity. Its April 2025 licensing regime was never suspended, rare-earth exports fell 9.6% in the first nine months and magnet shipments to the US fell 20% in August from July.

**Our reading.** Markets have treated the truce as settled: the yuan has strengthened about 9% since April 2025 to around 6.67 per dollar and the S&P 500 is near records. A lapse on 10 November is unlikely to be the base case, but the week after the election is when the paperwork has to catch up with the politics, and any slip would hit magnet-dependent manufacturers, chip supply chains and farm exports first.

## How the Truce Was Built

The arrangement dates from a fraught autumn. In October 2025 China expanded its rare-earth export controls, including a rule reaching foreign-made products containing more than 0.1% Chinese rare earths, and the US threatened an additional 100% tariff from 1 November. At Busan on 30 October 2025, Presidents Trump and Xi agreed to step back: the US cut its fentanyl tariff from 20% to 10%, lowering the average tariff on Chinese imports from about 57% to 47%, and suspended its affiliates rule and port fees; China suspended the October controls and its retaliatory measures for a year and committed to buy at least 25 million tonnes of US soybeans in each of 2026, 2027 and 2028. In May 2026 Trump visited Beijing, and Beijing reaffirmed the soybean pledge and agreed to buy 200 Boeing aircraft.

Xi’s state visit to Washington on 24-26 September produced the two-month extension, a new bilateral Board of Trade and an Investment Council, and reciprocal lists of about $30B of non-sensitive goods on each side eligible for lower tariffs. The asymmetry is telling: the US list covers 77 tariff lines, mainly consumer goods such as microwaves, while China’s covers more than 1,000 subheadings, including farm goods, seafood, cosmetics, medical devices and US coal. A planned 7.5% US overcapacity tariff was delayed. There was no jointly documented settlement on critical minerals.

## The Legal Calendar

Three dates follow the US election on 3 November. On 9 November the Federal Register notice suspending the Bureau of Industry and Security’s affiliates rule ends, so the rule applies again from 10 November unless BIS acts; CNBC reported it could reach about 20,000 Chinese entities. On 10 November MOFCOM Announcement No. 70 ends the suspension of China’s six October 2025 notices, which would restore licensing for five more rare earths, rare-earth equipment, battery materials and technology, and the 0.1% rule for foreign-made goods with Chinese rare-earth content. On 27 November Announcement No. 72, which suspended US-specific dual-use licensing, ends. A MOFCOM official said on 28 September that the arrangement had been extended to 10 January; as of 11 October no announcement changing the dates had been published, and as of 29 October neither side had published its notice.

Deadlines in the next ten weeks, and what changes if nothing is published:

- **3 Nov:** US midterm elections; political backdrop for any extension.
- **9 Nov:** BIS affiliates rule suspension ends; Entity List restrictions reach majority-owned affiliates from 10 Nov.
- **9-10 Nov:** US port-fee suspension ends; Section 301 fees on Chinese-linked ships could resume.
- **10 Nov:** MOFCOM No. 70 ends; Oct 2025 rare-earth controls and the 0.1% rule return.
- **27 Nov:** MOFCOM No. 72 ends; US-specific dual-use licensing returns.
- **10 Jan 2027:** political end of the truce; talks on a broader deal or another extension.
- **Jan 2027:** US targets on Chinese rare earths; Pentagon DFARS magnet cutoff; next round of US restrictions.

## Tariffs

The tariff wall has been rebuilt on different legal foundations. The Supreme Court’s ruling on 20 February voided the IEEPA tariffs, including the fentanyl and reciprocal layers on China. A 10% Section 122 surcharge filled the gap until it lapsed on 24 July, when a new Section 301 forced-labour tariff of 12.5% on most Chinese goods took effect. It stacks on top of the original Section 301 duties of 7.5% on List 4A and 25% on Lists 1 to 3, and on the four-year-review rates of 50% on legacy chips and solar cells and 100% on electric vehicles. For most products the result is roughly 20-40% plus the normal duty. PIIE estimates the average effective rate at 30% in 2025 and 24% in the first seven months of 2026, against 6% for imports from all other countries.

Source: PIIE, ‘Lost in tabulation’ (October 2026), based on US Census data.

## Trade Flows

The bilateral deficit has fallen steadily. Census data show the US goods deficit with China at $382 billion in 2022 and $202 billion in 2025; US imports from China fell 29.7% in 2025 and another 23% in the first half of 2026, while exports to China fell only 1%. China’s share of US imports, 21.6% in 2017, was about 9% in 2025, roughly where it stood when China joined the WTO in 2001. Stanford researchers find that the US decoupled from China, not from trade: total US imports grew 5.7% a year between 2017 and 2025 as Mexico, Taiwan and others took share, and China’s value added in US imports has proved stickier than the direct numbers suggest.

The fall may be overstated. From January to July 2026 the US recorded $158.3 billion of imports from China and Hong Kong, while China reported $258.1 billion of exports to the US, a gap of ~$100B, or 63% of US-reported imports, nearly five times its 2021-24 average. PIIE attributes much of it to tariff evasion: under-declared values, goods routed through third countries and low-value parcels. The Coalition for a Prosperous America argues that in March the deficit fell 46% largely because declared values dropped, not quantities.

Sources: US Census Bureau; Congressional Research Service; Stanford SCCEI; PIIE (October 2026), using US Census and China customs data.

## Rare Earths

Rare earths are the reason the truce matters to industry. China controls an estimated 90% of global rare-earth processing and magnet fabrication. The suspension covers only the October 2025 additions; the April 2025 regime, which requires case-by-case licences for samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium and yttrium and their magnets, has never been suspended. Flows reflect that: China’s rare-earth exports fell 9.6% to 43,711 tonnes in January-September, and magnet shipments to the US fell to 512 tonnes in August, down 20% from July and 13% from a year earlier, according to Chinese customs data reported by the Financial Times. If the October rules return, any product anywhere containing more than 0.1% Chinese rare earths by value would need a Chinese licence, which would reach US defence, auto and electronics supply chains directly.

Sources: BISI, September 2026 (estimate); China customs via Technology.org (exports, Jan-Sep); Financial Times via CNBC (magnets, August).

## Purchases and the $30 Billion Lists

Purchase commitments are the most visible scorecard. Reuters reported that China had bought almost half of its 25-million-tonne soybean commitment for 2026 by late September, after buying about a million tonnes in a single week; delivery on the 200 Boeing aircraft and on rare-earth access has been harder to verify. The $30 billion reciprocal tariff lists agreed in September are small relative to $415 billion of two-way goods trade in 2025, but they matter as a template: the Board of Trade’s first task is to implement them, with an agricultural working group and US coal added to China’s reduced-duty regime for 2027 and 2028.

Source: Reuters via CNBC (late September 2026); White House fact sheet (commitment).

## Markets

Financial markets have priced the truce as durable. The yuan, which weakened to 7.34 per dollar after the April 2025 tariff escalation, has strengthened steadily since Busan to about 6.67, its firmest level in the period shown, as China’s exports to other markets held up. The S&P 500 recovered from the spring 2025 tariff shock to records, led by technology. The sectors most exposed to a lapse are those that depend on Chinese magnets and battery materials (autos, defence, robotics, electronics), US farm exporters, shippers exposed to port fees and chip companies affected by the affiliates rule. A slip on 9-10 November would test that calm; a smooth publication of notices would confirm it.

Sources: Federal Reserve H.10 daily exchange rates, to 23 October 2026; S&P 500 monthly averages (Shiller data via public dataset) to September 2026.
