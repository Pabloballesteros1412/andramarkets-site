## Executive Summary

**Demand is not the constraint.** Management described its outlook as limited by supply rather than demand. Chief financial officer Colette Kress said supply will remain a bottleneck “at least through the end of fiscal year 2028”, and chief executive Jensen Huang said the 70 per cent growth outlook for next year reflects what supply allows; “the unconstrained would be a lot higher.”

**Margins are past their peak.** The gross margin of 75 per cent is guided to fall to 74 per cent this quarter and to a low of 71 to 72 per cent in the fourth, mainly because memory chips have become much more expensive, before settling at 72 to 73 per cent next year. It remains one of the highest margins of any hardware company in the world.

**The valuation has come down to earth.** At about 20 times forward earnings, Nvidia trades below most of the large chip companies covered in this series, because analysts expect earnings per share to rise by around 70 per cent next year. The debate has shifted from growth to durability: how long can AI spending keep rising, and how much of the market will Nvidia keep as customers develop their own chips.

## Business Overview

Nvidia designs graphics processors (GPUs) and the systems built around them, but does not manufacture them; production is outsourced mainly to TSMC. Its data centre business sells complete AI systems, from single chips to full racks of 72 GPUs connected by its own networking technology, together with the CUDA software platform that most AI developers use. From this quarter the company reports two segments: Data Center, now 93 per cent of revenue, and Edge Computing, which includes gaming chips, cars and robotics.

Within data centres, sales are split between the large cloud providers (hyperscalers), which brought in $49 billion in the quarter, and a second group of AI labs, governments, enterprises and specialised clouds, which brought in $40 billion and grew faster, at 138 per cent. The second group is the key to diversification: today three direct customers account for 44 per cent of revenue.

## Second-Quarter Results

Revenue of $96.2 billion beat expectations of about $92.2 billion, and non-GAAP earnings per share of $2.22 compared with forecasts of about $2.10. Data centre networking grew 18 per cent on the quarter, and the Spectrum-X Ethernet business grew 2.6 times in a year. Operating income was $63.7 billion and free cash flow $21.3 billion. GAAP net income of $59.7 billion included $7.8 billion of gains on equity stakes in other companies, which do not reflect the operating business.

## Product Cycle and Supply

The next generation of chips, Vera Rubin, began shipping in August, and management expects it to be the fastest product ramp in the company’s history, accounting for about 20 per cent of data centre revenue in the third quarter. To secure components, Nvidia has raised its supply commitments from about $119 billion to $279 billion, mostly for memory, and signed a multiyear memory partnership with SK hynix. Customer commitments are equally large: Amazon Web Services will deploy two million more Nvidia GPUs, and OpenAI’s existing and planned commitments total about 12 gigawatts of Nvidia computing.

## Capital Allocation

Nvidia returned $26 billion to shareholders in the quarter, including $19.7 billion of share buybacks, and on 28 September added $150 billion to its buyback authorisation, leaving $235 billion available, about 4 per cent of its market value on our calculation. It is also building a financing platform with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR that aims to mobilise more than $500 billion of outside capital to fund data centres using its chips. Huang described this as “the first time that technology chips are an investable asset class”.

## Valuation

At $237.47 Nvidia is worth $5.73 trillion, one of the most valuable listed companies in the world. It trades at 30 times trailing earnings but about 19.6 times forward earnings on StockAnalysis’s measure, and analysts expect earnings per share to rise from about $9.31 this fiscal year to $15.91 next year. Of 61 analysts, 58 rate the shares a buy, with an average target of $328.72, about 38 per cent above the current price. The shares reached a record close of $239.24 on 6 October, after Morgan Stanley named Nvidia its top chip pick and the buyback was enlarged, but have lagged the sector this year as investors rotated into memory and equipment makers.

## Risk Considerations

The main risk is concentration: three customers account for 44 per cent of revenue, and they are also developing alternatives. Google’s TPU, Amazon’s Trainium and Meta’s chips, many designed with Broadcom, compete directly, and AMD has commitments for its MI450 chips from OpenAI, Meta and Anthropic. A second risk is financing: much AI investment is now funded by debt and by deals in which suppliers invest in their customers, and the cost of insuring Nvidia’s debt against default reached a record in late July. If AI spending slows, the supply commitments that protect Nvidia today would become a burden. Higher memory costs, power shortages for data centres and export rules are further risks.

## Outlook and Monitoring Points

The next results are expected around 18 November. The key indicators are whether revenue reaches the $108 billion guidance, the speed of the Rubin ramp, the gross margin trend as memory costs rise, and the share of revenue from customers outside the large cloud providers. Results from TSMC on 15 October and AMD on 3 November will offer early evidence on AI demand. Nvidia remains the core of the AI build-out; the question for investors is no longer whether it grows, but for how long.
