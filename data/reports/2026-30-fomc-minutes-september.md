**Coverage summary.** The minutes of the Federal Open Market Committee meeting of 15 and 16 September, published on 7 October, confirm a 25 basis point increase in the federal funds target range to 3.75% to 4.00%, approved by all twelve voting members. Inflation has moved further from the 2% objective, with the personal consumption expenditures price index rising 3.8% in the year to August and 3.4% excluding food and energy, pushed up by tariffs, higher energy prices linked to disruption of oil shipments from the Middle East, and rising prices for goods tied to the AI investment boom. The staff now see inflation returning to 2% only in 2029. Most participants judged that a further increase would likely be appropriate by the end of the year, yet the record shows a split between officials who see the tightening as insurance against unanchored expectations and those who see it as a response to demand running ahead of supply. This note reviews the decision, the committee’s assessment of the economy and inflation, its reading of financial conditions, and what the minutes imply for policy and markets.

## Key points from the minutes

- **Decision**: +25bp to 3.75% to 4.00%; unanimous, 12 to 0; effective 17 September.
- **Administered rates**: IORB 3.90%; ON RRP 3.75%; primary credit and standing repo 4.00%.
- **Balance sheet**: reserve management purchases paused; “not on a preset course”.
- **Inflation, August**: PCE 3.8%; core 3.4%; 3.6% and 3.2% on the new BEA method.
- **Staff forecast**: inflation back to 2% only by 2029; revised up; risks skewed to the upside.
- **Labor market**: unemployment 4.1%; risks to employment broadly balanced.
- **Financial conditions**: 2 to 10 year yields up about 35bp; mostly real rates; dollar weaker.
- **Guidance**: “Most” see another increase by year end; next meeting 27 and 28 October.
- **Market pricing**: October hold; December increase.

## Executive Summary

**A unanimous vote with divided reasoning.** All twelve voters supported the September increase, but the minutes describe two distinct justifications. Many participants framed a higher policy path as prudent risk management, arguing that the longer inflation stays above target, the greater the danger that households and firms stop believing in the 2% objective. A number of participants went further and judged that higher rates were necessary on their central outlook, because demand, supported by AI investment and solid growth, is pressing against the economy’s capacity. The difference matters: the first camp could stop after one or two increases if inflation expectations hold, while the second would need evidence that demand is cooling.

**The bias is towards one more increase.** Most participants assessed that another increase would likely be appropriate by year end, and several considered policy “not restrictive or only mildly restrictive”, while a couple raised their estimates of the neutral rate. The committee has therefore not signalled a pause; it has signalled that the question is when, not whether, absent a clear change in the data. Markets read the document as consistent with a hold in October and an increase in December, and pared bets on back to back moves.

**The inflation picture has several sources at once.** Tariffs, energy and AI-related goods are each lifting prices, and the staff forecast now sees inflation returning to 2% only in 2029. Some participants warned that more than five consecutive years above target could unanchor expectations. With unemployment at 4.1% and growth expected to outpace potential through 2028, the Fed sees little in the real economy that argues for patience, which is why the burden of proof has shifted to the doves.

## The Policy Decision

The committee raised the target range for the federal funds rate by 25 basis points to 3.75% to 4.00%, effective 17 September. To implement the decision, the Board raised the interest rate on reserve balances to 3.90% and the primary credit rate to 4.00%, while the committee set the standing overnight repurchase agreement rate at 4.00% and the overnight reverse repurchase rate at 3.75%, the bottom of the range. The structure of administered rates is therefore unchanged in relation to the target range, and money market rates moved up in line with the decision.

On the balance sheet, the committee paused the reserve management purchases of Treasury bills it had been conducting to keep reserves ample, and the minutes stress that the balance sheet is “not on a preset course”. The pause removes a modest source of demand for short-dated Treasury securities and, together with the rate increase, makes clear that the committee does not want its balance sheet operations to be read as easing at a time when it is tightening through rates.

## Economic Assessment

The staff and participants described an economy that continues to grow at a solid pace. Real GDP growth has held up through the year, and the staff projection has output growing faster than potential through 2028. That projection is central to the hawkish case: if the economy expands faster than its capacity for three more years, the gap between demand and supply is likely to widen rather than close, adding to price pressures that already come from other sources.

The labor market looks stable rather than tight. The unemployment rate stood at 4.1%, and participants judged that the risks to employment are now broadly balanced, a notable shift from earlier in the cycle, when the committee was more concerned about a weakening in hiring. That change frees the committee to focus on inflation. Several participants noted that further tariff increases represent an upside risk to prices.

## The Inflation Debate

Inflation is the reason for the tightening, and the minutes make clear that it is not coming from a single place. The PCE price index rose 3.8% in the twelve months to August and 3.4% excluding food and energy. On the Bureau of Economic Analysis’s new methodology, the figures are slightly lower at 3.6% and 3.2%, but the conclusion is the same: inflation is well above the 2% objective and has been moving away from it. Participants attributed the pressure to tariffs passing through to goods prices, to higher energy costs after disruption to oil shipments from the Middle East, and to rising prices for goods tied to the AI buildout, such as semiconductors, servers and electrical equipment.

Many participants observed that the longer energy prices remain elevated, the greater the risk that they spread into the prices of other goods and services and into wage setting. Some warned that the AI investment wave could cause demand to outpace supply more broadly, a concern that turns what was previously regarded as a supply-side story about productivity into a demand-side inflation risk. The staff revised up their inflation forecast and now see PCE inflation returning to 2% only in 2029, with risks skewed to the upside. Some participants noted that inflation would by then have been above target for more than five years, a period long enough, in their view, to put the anchoring of expectations at risk.

## Financial Conditions

Financial conditions tightened over the intermeeting period. Treasury yields between two and ten years rose by about 35 basis points, and the minutes attribute most of the increase to higher real rates rather than to inflation compensation, which suggests investors were pricing a tighter policy path rather than losing confidence in the inflation objective. The manager’s report cited four drivers: expectations for the policy path, geopolitical developments, uncertainty about the Treasury’s buyback operations, and “competition for capital from heavy private debt issuance” to fund AI investment. The last point is new in the Fed’s record and reflects the scale of bond issuance by hyperscalers and data center developers discussed in our notes on Oracle, Alphabet and SpaceX.

Two other observations stand out. The dollar depreciated over the period, partly after coordinated intervention by the United States and Japan in support of the yen, which eased conditions at the margin and adds to import prices. And equity gains over the period came entirely from earnings growth, with price to earnings multiples declining. In other words, rising rates are already compressing valuations, and stock prices are holding up because profits, led by technology and AI-related companies, continue to grow quickly.

## Policy Outlook

The minutes use a conventional vocabulary to indicate how widely a view is held, from “most” at the top, through “many”, “a number”, “several” and “some”, to “a couple”. Read in that light, the document is clearly hawkish. The view that another increase would likely be appropriate by year end carries the strongest qualifier in the text. The risk management argument for a higher path is held by many participants, and the stronger claim that a higher path is needed on the central outlook by a number of them. Several regarded current policy as not restrictive or only mildly restrictive, and a couple raised their estimate of the neutral rate, the level at which policy neither stimulates nor restrains the economy. No group of participants is recorded as arguing for an early end to tightening.

The split over reasoning will shape what comes next. Officials who favour precautionary tightening are likely to look mainly at inflation expectations and at whether energy and tariff effects spread into services and wages; if those remain contained, they could be satisfied with one more move. Officials who see excess demand will look at growth, investment and the labor market, and could support a longer sequence of increases if activity stays strong. Contemporaneous reporting described the October meeting as likely to feature a vigorous debate, with the possibility of more than one dissent, an unusual prospect after a unanimous vote.

## Market Implications

Markets interpreted the minutes as confirming the direction of policy without accelerating it. Futures pricing pared the probability of back to back increases and now points to an unchanged rate at the 27 and 28 October meeting, followed by a 25 basis point increase at the meeting of 8 and 9 December, which would take the target range to 4.00% to 4.25%. That path is consistent with the “by year end” language in the minutes and gives the committee time to see two more inflation reports and the October employment data before acting.

For bonds, the message is that the front end has less room to fall and that real yields, already the main driver of the rise in longer rates, are unlikely to retreat while the committee leans towards tightening and private borrowing for AI remains heavy. For equities, the minutes confirm what the intermeeting period already showed: multiples are contracting, and returns depend on earnings growth continuing to outrun the rise in discount rates. Rate-sensitive sectors such as housing, utilities and smaller companies with floating-rate debt remain the most exposed. For the dollar, a hawkish Fed would normally be supportive, but the minutes’ reference to coordinated yen intervention is a reminder that currency policy is now an active consideration alongside interest rates.

## Risk Considerations

The main upside risk to rates is a broadening of inflation. If energy prices stay high, if further tariff increases materialise, or if AI-related demand keeps pushing up prices for equipment and electricity, the precautionary and demand-driven camps could converge on a longer tightening cycle than markets now price. A rise in measured inflation expectations would be the clearest trigger, given the weight participants placed on the risk of five or more years above target.

The downside risks are of a different kind. A sharp correction in AI-related investment or in the financial markets that fund it would weaken both the growth outlook and the demand-side argument for tighter policy, and the committee’s assessment that labor market risks are balanced could change quickly if hiring slowed. A de-escalation in the Middle East and a fall in oil prices would ease headline inflation and strengthen the case of those who see the tightening as insurance rather than necessity. Finally, a committee divided on its reasoning is harder to forecast: dissents in October would signal that the consensus behind further increases is narrower than the “most participants” language suggests.

## Outlook and Monitoring Points

The key data before the October meeting are the September consumer price index and PCE reports, the September employment report and measures of inflation expectations from surveys and inflation-linked bonds. Within those, the most informative signals will be services inflation outside energy, which would show whether price pressures are broadening, and wage growth, which would show whether the labor market is adding to them. Oil prices and the course of the Middle East disruption, any new tariff announcements, and the pace of corporate bond issuance tied to AI will also shape the committee’s view. On current evidence, the Fed has moved from asking whether to tighten to asking how far, and the October debate over why it is tightening will determine the answer.
