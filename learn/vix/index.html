What moves the VIX

What the volatility index measures, why it jumps when stocks fall, and what it cannot tell you.

3 min read

What the VIX measures

The Cboe Volatility Index, the VIX, is a measure of how much the S&P 500 is expected to move over the next 30 days. It is not calculated from past prices. Cboe derives it from the prices of S&P 500 options across a wide range of strike prices, using the fact that an option is worth more when bigger moves are expected. The result is quoted in annualised percentage points.

A rough way to read the number: divide it by the square root of 12 to get the expected size of a one-month move. A VIX of 20 implies a typical move, one standard deviation, of about 5.8% over the next month, up or down. The VIX says nothing about direction. It prices the size of the move.

Why it jumps when stocks fall

The VIX usually rises when the S&P 500 falls and drifts lower when stocks climb steadily. Several forces push the same way. Falling prices tend to be followed by larger daily swings, so realised volatility rises. Investors who own stocks buy put options to protect themselves after a fall, and that demand raises option prices. Lower share prices also leave companies more leveraged, which makes their equity riskier.

Rallies rarely have the same effect. Steady gains tend to come with small daily moves and little demand for protection. That asymmetry is why the VIX is often called the fear gauge, even though it is a price of insurance rather than a survey of anyone's feelings.

The other things that move it

Scheduled events. Option prices rise ahead of known risks, such as a Federal Reserve decision, a consumer price index release or the results of the largest companies, and often fall back once the event has passed. A drop in the VIX the day after a big release is common and usually means the uncertainty was resolved, not that the outlook improved.

The volatility risk premium. Implied volatility has usually been higher than the volatility that actually follows, because option sellers demand compensation for the risk of a crash. Academic studies of this variance risk premium find it is persistent and that it widens in stressed markets. A VIX of 15 does not mean the market will move 15% annualised; on average it has moved less.

Mean reversion. Spikes in the VIX have tended to fade, and very low readings have tended not to last. That is a tendency in the history, not a rule, and it says nothing about timing.

The term structure

The VIX looks 30 days ahead. Cboe publishes sister indices over other horizons, including nine days (VIX9D), three months (VIX3M) and six months (VIX6M). Plotted together they form a term structure. In calm markets it slopes upward, called contango: little is expected to happen soon, and more uncertainty is priced further out. In stress it inverts, called backwardation: near-term volatility is priced above longer-term volatility because the danger is now. The shape often says more than the level of any one index.

What it cannot tell you

The VIX is not a forecast of returns, and a high reading is not a signal to buy or sell. It cannot be bought directly: investors trade VIX futures and options, whose prices follow where the index is expected to be at expiry, not where it is today, and products built on futures can lose value over time when the curve is in contango. The index also covers only the S&P 500. Other markets have their own measures, such as the MOVE index for Treasury yields.
