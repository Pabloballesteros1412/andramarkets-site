What credit spreads signal

The extra yield companies pay over the government, and why it widens before trouble.

3 min read

The extra yield

When a company borrows by selling bonds, it pays more than the US government does for the same length of time. The difference between the yield on a corporate bond and the yield on a Treasury of the same maturity is its credit spread, usually quoted in percentage points or basis points (one basis point is a hundredth of a percentage point).

Many corporate bonds can be repaid early at the company's choice, which changes how they behave. The option-adjusted spread, OAS, strips out the value of that option so that bonds with and without it can be compared. The ICE BofA index spreads shown on the dashboard are option-adjusted.

What the spread pays for

The spread compensates the lender for several things. The first is expected loss: the chance the company defaults, times how much of the money is lost if it does. The second is liquidity: corporate bonds trade less easily than Treasuries, and selling a large position in a hurry can be costly. The third is a risk premium, the extra return investors demand for holding risk that tends to hurt at the worst times, because defaults rise together in recessions.

Research has found that expected default losses explain only part of the level of spreads. The rest, the premium, moves with investors' appetite for risk, which is why spreads can widen sharply even when few companies are actually defaulting.

Investment grade and high yield

Rating agencies grade bonds by credit quality. Bonds rated BBB minus or above (Baa3 at one agency) are investment grade; anything below is high yield, sometimes called junk. Investment grade companies are mostly large and stable, and their spreads are measured in tenths of a percentage point to a little over one point in normal times. High yield issuers are smaller or more indebted, and their spreads are several times larger and far more volatile.

Because defaults are rare among investment grade issuers, their spreads mostly reflect liquidity and the risk premium. High yield spreads respond more directly to the outlook for defaults, and so to the economy.

Why markets watch them

Credit investors are paid back at most in full, so they gain little when things go well and lose a lot when they go badly. That makes the credit market quick to react to signs of trouble. A sudden widening in high yield spreads, without a matching move in Treasuries, is often an early sign of stress.

Academic research supports the signal. A study published in the American Economic Review in 2012 separated corporate bond spreads into a part explained by expected defaults and an excess bond premium, the part that reflects lenders' willingness to take risk. A rising excess premium was followed by weaker investment and output. The Federal Reserve tracks spreads and this premium in its assessment of financial stability.

Spreads also matter in their own right. They set what companies pay to borrow, so wider spreads tighten financial conditions even if the Fed has not moved.

What they do not tell you

Very tight spreads are not a guarantee of safety. They can mean investors are reaching for yield and being paid little for the risk they take. Index spreads also change with the mix of bonds in the index: if many lower-rated issuers are upgraded or default and leave, the average moves for reasons that have little to do with the market. And the spread is only one part of a company's borrowing cost. When Treasury yields rise, a company can pay much more to borrow even with an unchanged spread.

Reading it on the dashboard

The credit spreads dashboard plots the ICE BofA US High Yield and US Corporate (investment grade) option-adjusted spreads over the chosen range. Look at the direction more than the level, and at whether high yield is moving faster than investment grade: a widening led by high yield is the classic sign of rising concern about defaults.
