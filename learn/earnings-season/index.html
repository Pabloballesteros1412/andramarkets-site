How earnings season works

When companies report, what is in the release, and why guidance often matters more than the beat.

3 min read

Four times a year

Listed companies in the United States report their results every quarter. The formal report is a filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission: Form 10-Q after each of the first three fiscal quarters and Form 10-K after the full year. The largest companies must file the 10-Q within 40 days of the quarter's end and the 10-K within 60 days; smaller companies have longer.

Most companies announce their numbers before the filing, in a press release furnished to the SEC on Form 8-K, often followed the same day by a conference call with analysts. Because most companies close their quarters at the end of March, June, September and December, the releases bunch together. Earnings season usually starts with the large banks a couple of weeks after the quarter ends, and most of the S&P 500 has reported within about six weeks.

What is in a release

Revenue, profit and earnings per share for the quarter, against the same quarter a year earlier. Most companies also report adjusted figures that leave out items such as restructuring charges or the cost of share-based pay. SEC rules require any such non-GAAP measure to be reconciled to the closest measure under US accounting standards, so the reader can see what was left out and why.

Many companies also give guidance: their own expectation for revenue, margins or earnings in the next quarter or the full year. On the call, management explains the numbers and answers questions. The call often moves the shares as much as the release itself.

Before the open, after the close

Most US companies release results either before the market opens or after it closes, so that investors have time to read them before they can trade on them in the regular session. Under the SEC's Regulation FD, material information has to be made public to everyone at once, not shared first with selected analysts or investors. That is why results arrive as a press release and a public call rather than in private meetings.

Estimates, beats and misses

Analysts at banks and research firms publish forecasts for each company. The average of those forecasts is the consensus, and a result above it is a beat, below it a miss. Two habits make a beat less informative than it sounds. Estimates are often trimmed in the weeks before a release, and companies with some discretion over their guidance tend to set expectations they can clear. Over many seasons a clear majority of S&P 500 companies have reported earnings above consensus.

So the market tends to judge a result by more than the headline. Revenue tells you about demand, which is harder to manage than costs. Margins show whether growth is profitable. Guidance shows what the company itself expects next, and a beat paired with weak guidance is often met with a falling share price.

Reading the reaction

A useful measure of how a result was received is the move in the shares on the first full trading session after the release, set against the move in the wider market that day. A stock that falls 2% on a day the index falls 2% was not punished for its results. Moves are often largest in the first minutes after the release, outside regular hours, when fewer shares trade and prices can overshoot.

Earnings season also gives a reading of the economy as a whole. Banks describe borrowers and deposits, retailers describe the consumer, manufacturers describe orders. The aggregate figure, the growth in earnings for the index as a whole, is one of the main inputs into how investors value the market.

Reading it on the calendar
