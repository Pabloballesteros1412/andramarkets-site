How the Fed sets rates

Who decides, what the target range is, and how the Fed makes the market follow it.

3 min read

The mandate

Congress has given the Federal Reserve two goals for monetary policy: maximum employment and stable prices, along with moderate long-term interest rates. The Fed has defined stable prices as inflation of 2% a year, measured by the price index for personal consumption expenditures. Its main tool for pursuing both goals is the level of short-term interest rates.

Who decides

Rates are set by the Federal Open Market Committee, the FOMC. It has twelve voting members: the seven governors of the Federal Reserve Board in Washington, the president of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, and four of the other eleven Reserve Bank presidents, who take turns a year at a time. All twelve Reserve Bank presidents take part in the discussion. The committee holds eight scheduled meetings a year and can meet in between if it needs to.

What it sets

The FOMC sets a target range for the federal funds rate, the rate at which banks lend reserves to each other overnight. The range is a quarter of a percentage point wide, for example 4.25% to 4.50%. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York publishes the rate actually paid each day, the effective federal funds rate, calculated from reported overnight loans as a volume-weighted median.

How the market follows it

The Fed does not order banks to lend at a given rate. Today it steers the market with rates it pays and charges itself. It pays interest on the reserves banks hold at the Fed. No bank should lend reserves for much less than it can earn by leaving them at the Fed, so this rate pulls the market toward the target range. Through its overnight reverse repurchase facility the Fed also borrows from a wider set of institutions, including money market funds, at a rate that sets a floor under short-term rates. Banks can borrow from the Fed at the discount window at a rate above the range.

This works because the banking system holds ample reserves. Before 2008 reserves were scarce, and the Fed hit its target by adding or draining small amounts of reserves each day through open market operations. Now it moves the rates it administers and the market rate follows.

Decision day

At the end of each meeting the committee releases a statement, at 2:00 pm New York time, giving the decision, a short assessment of the economy and how each member voted. The Chair holds a press conference half an hour later. At four meetings a year, in March, June, September and December, participants also publish their economic projections, including the dot plot: each participant's view of the appropriate rate at the end of the next few years, shown as a dot. The dots are individual views, not a committee plan. Minutes follow three weeks after each meeting.

From the overnight rate to the economy

A change in the overnight rate reaches the economy through other prices. Bank lending rates and money market yields move almost at once. Longer rates, such as mortgage rates and corporate bond yields, move with the expected path of policy, which is why the Fed's guidance about future meetings can matter as much as the decision itself. Asset prices and the dollar respond too. The effect on spending, hiring and inflation builds over many months, which is why the Fed has to act on forecasts rather than on today's data.

The Fed also has its balance sheet. By buying Treasury and mortgage-backed securities it can push down long-term yields when short-term rates are already near zero, and by letting them mature it can do the opposite.

Reading it on the dashboard

The Fed path dashboard turns 30-day federal funds futures into the average overnight rate the market expects month by month: 100 minus the futures price. A path sloping down prices cuts, one sloping up prices hikes. The dashed line shows the path at the start of the range, so you can see how expectations have shifted. Futures include a small risk premium, so the path is a guide to market expectations, not a forecast of what the committee will do.
